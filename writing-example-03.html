I Install Heat Pumps for a Living. Here's What I Actually Tell People Who Think They Don't Work.

Air source heat pumps extract heat from outside air and operate efficiently down to minus 15 degrees Celsius. Performance declines as temperatures fall — a real-world coefficient of performance of around 3 at freezing compared to 5 at 8 degrees — but most UK winter days sit between 5 and 10 degrees, where efficiency is strong. For a well-insulated home with appropriately sized radiators and a correctly calculated system, a heat pump is now a reliable, efficient alternative to a gas boiler.

The question we get most often, standing in someone's kitchen with a clipboard and a heat loss survey form, is some version of this: do air source heat pumps actually work in UK winters, or is this just something that works down south? We have been installing air source heat pumps for over two decades — before the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, before the marketing campaigns, before most of the national installers knew what a heat loss calculation was. We have seen what works and what does not. This article sets out the honest answers to the five objections we hear most often.

Heat Pumps Are Part of a Heating System, Not a Replacement for One

The first thing we say to anyone who calls about an air source heat pump is that the system matters as much as the unit. An air source heat pump in a poorly insulated house with undersized radiators, running at a high flow temperature, will be expensive to run and will struggle to keep the rooms warm. That is not a heat pump problem. That is a system design problem. It is also, unfortunately, what happens when an installer quotes a heat pump from a postcode and a meter reading rather than a heat loss calculation done on site.

The only honest way to establish what a property actually needs is a heat loss calculation done on site: wall areas, window areas, insulation type, floor construction, ceiling height, and the condition of the existing radiators. Under MCS rules, a heat pump must be sized to meet the full heat demand of the property. The number that comes out of the calculation is the kilowatt rating the system needs. A guess from a postcode will often be wrong. Sometimes too large, sometimes too small. Both produce bad outcomes over the fifteen years the system is running.

What MCS requires on every air source heat pump installation

The Microgeneration Certification Scheme requires that every heat pump be installed to meet the full heat demand of the property it is heating. That means a heat loss calculation on site, room by room, before the system is sized. It is not optional if the installation is to qualify for the Boiler Upgrade Scheme. We do this calculation on every survey visit as a matter of course. It is the only basis on which we will quote a heat pump size.

UK Winters and Heat Pump Efficiency: Do Heat Pumps Work in Winter?

The question of whether heat pumps work in winter — specifically whether air source heat pump cold weather performance holds up in a typical UK climate — comes up in almost every conversation. It has a grain of truth and a large amount of misunderstanding wrapped around it. According to real-world UK winter heat pump performance data from Energy Stats UK, a heat pump running at 8 degrees Celsius outside air temperature can convert 1 kilowatt hour of electricity into approximately 5 kilowatt hours of heat. At 0 degrees, the same system produces around 3 kilowatt hours per kilowatt hour of electricity. Performance drops as temperatures fall. That part is true.

What the objection leaves out is where most UK winter days actually land. The majority of days from October to March sit between 5 and 10 degrees Celsius. The handful of genuinely cold days — temperatures of minus 5 and below — are real but not representative. Modern air source heat pumps are designed to operate down to minus 15 degrees. They do not stop. They work harder and use more electricity on the coldest days, just as a gas boiler runs longer on the same days. The seasonal coefficient of performance (the SCOP) reflects the whole heating season, not just the worst days. For a correctly designed system, the SCOP is typically between 2.8 and 3.5, depending on the flow temperature the system is designed to run at.

The obstacle, when there is one, is system design — which brings us back to the heat loss calculation.

Radiators or Underfloor Heating: What Your Heat Pump Actually Needs

A common piece of advice circulating online is that heat pumps only work with underfloor heating. This is not accurate as a blanket statement. Modern air source heat pumps can run at higher flow temperatures of 55 to 60 degrees Celsius and deliver heat through existing radiators, provided those radiators are large enough to transfer the required heat at the lower flow temperature the system runs at most of the time. The confusion comes from the fact that heat pumps run most efficiently at lower flow temperatures, where underfloor heating is a natural fit. But efficiency and functionality are not the same thing.

In practice, the picture is usually this: some radiators in some rooms need to be upsized. Rarely the whole house. A bedroom with an older single-panel radiator and a reasonable level of insulation will often need a larger panel. A living room with a large existing double-panel radiator may need nothing. The heat loss calculation tells us which rooms need attention, and what size radiator will work at the flow temperature we design the system to run at. We go through this room by room at survey. The answer is property-specific. No blanket rule applies.

Underfloor heating works particularly well with heat pumps because it distributes heat over a large surface area at a low flow temperature. If you have it, or if you are doing significant floor work, it is worth considering. But it is not a prerequisite for a well-performing heat pump installation.

Running Costs: an Honest Answer

The running cost question causes most confusion, and the honest answer is that it depends on three things: the SCOP of the installed system, the quality of the property's insulation, and the electricity tariff you are on. At standard electricity tariff rates, electricity costs more per kilowatt hour than gas. A heat pump's efficiency multiplier of 3 to 5 units of heat per unit of electricity offsets much of that difference, but at standard tariff rates the gap is narrow for well-insulated homes and can tip the wrong way for poorly insulated ones.

Dedicated heat pump tariffs change the equation. Off-peak electricity rates available on tariffs like Octopus Cosy or similar heat pump tariffs bring the running cost per unit of heat below the equivalent gas cost. This is not a niche benefit. It is increasingly the standard advice for heat pump households. Switching tariff after installation is an important step, and we discuss it with every customer at survey.

We do not give specific annual savings figures at survey. Any figure we quoted would depend on your current boiler's efficiency, your household's pattern of use, your insulation, and the tariff you move to — all of which vary. What we can tell you is the SCOP the system we design for your property should achieve, and the heat demand it is designed to meet. You can calculate the rest from your electricity supplier's tariff, or we can walk through it with you.

If the numbers do not work for your house, we say so. We are not going to fit a system that leaves you with higher bills and a poor experience.

How the Boiler Upgrade Scheme Works

The Boiler Upgrade Scheme is a government grant programme that pays £7,500 towards the cost of an air source heat pump installation. It is administered by Ofgem and runs until at least March 2028. From July 2026, properties not connected to the gas grid are eligible for a higher grant of £9,000, as confirmed by the Energy Saving Trust's guide to the Boiler Upgrade Scheme. The grant is deducted from the invoice. You never receive the money directly and you never pay the full amount upfront.

Eligibility is straightforward for most homeowners. According to the Boiler Upgrade Scheme eligibility page on GOV.UK, you need to own the property and be replacing an existing fossil fuel heating system — gas, oil, LPG, or direct electric. Your installer must be MCS certified. The installer submits the voucher application on your behalf. You do not need to navigate Ofgem's portal or track submission deadlines.

What to Look for in an Air Source Heat Pump Installer

MCS certification is the minimum requirement for any air source heat pump installer. You can check any installer's certification status on the MCS certified installer search on mcscertified.com. MCS certification is also a prerequisite for the Boiler Upgrade Scheme. Without an MCS-certified installer, the grant is not available. Beyond that, the question that matters most is whether the installer does a heat loss calculation on site before quoting a system size. If they offer a quote from a phone call or a postcode, that is not a properly designed installation. Walk away.

Heat pump installation requires experience that is genuinely hard to acquire quickly. A company that entered the market in 2022 when the BUS grant launched has three years of installations and no track record on how those systems perform in year eight or year ten. That gap matters for a system with a 20-year design life. We are not saying newer installers are bad. We are saying that experience is a legitimate thing to ask about.

Our model is continuity. The same engineering team installs and services, and where possible the same engineer returns for the annual service. Most of our heat pump service customers see the same engineer year after year. Annual heat pump servicing is available as a one-off or as part of a service plan.

The Noise Question

Air source heat pumps do make noise. The outdoor unit contains a fan and a compressor, and both are audible. A modern unit runs at around 40 to 50 decibels at a one-metre distance — roughly equivalent to a quiet conversation or a refrigerator running in the next room. It is not silent. If your garden is small and a neighbour's bedroom window is close to where the unit would need to go, siting is something to think about carefully. We look at this as part of every survey, and we will tell you if siting is going to be a problem at your property before anything is agreed.

Permitted Development rules for heat pump installation require the unit to be sited at least one metre from the property boundary and below certain noise thresholds at the nearest neighbour's window. Most residential properties can accommodate a unit without difficulty. The challenge we see most often is a narrow side passage running between two properties. In those cases, the unit goes on the rear of the property and we design the pipework run accordingly. If yours cannot be sited cleanly, we say so at survey — not after the scaffolding goes up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do air source heat pumps work in snow?

Yes. Air source heat pumps are designed to operate in all weather conditions, including snow, frost, and ice. The outdoor unit's defrost cycle handles ice buildup on the heat exchanger automatically. You may see a small cloud of vapour from the unit during a defrost cycle, which is normal. Performance is lower when outdoor temperatures are at or below freezing, but modern units continue to extract heat from the air and provide effective heating down to minus 15 degrees Celsius.

Can I keep my existing radiators when I install a heat pump?

In many cases, yes. Whether your existing radiators can stay depends on their size relative to the heat output needed in each room at the flow temperature the heat pump is designed to run at. A heat loss calculation on site determines room by room which radiators are adequate and which need upgrading. In a typical UK home, some rooms need larger radiators and others do not. It is rarely the whole house. Any radiator upgrades required are included in the system design and quoted alongside the installation.

What is the Seasonal Coefficient of Performance and why does it matter?

The Seasonal Coefficient of Performance (SCOP) is the average efficiency of a heat pump across a whole heating season, accounting for varying outdoor temperatures throughout the year. It gives a more accurate picture of real-world performance than the COP figure quoted in manufacturer brochures, which is measured under specific test conditions. A SCOP of 3.0 means the system produces 3 kilowatt hours of heat for every 1 kilowatt hour of electricity consumed, on average, across the heating season. For a well-designed installation, SCOP typically falls between 2.8 and 3.5. Higher SCOP requires lower flow temperatures, which means well-sized radiators or underfloor heating and good insulation.

Is my home suitable for a heat pump?

The only honest answer to this question comes from a heat loss survey on site. The things that matter are insulation, radiator sizing, available external space for the outdoor unit, and whether you have access to an electricity supply sufficient for the system. Some properties with solid walls and limited insulation need fabric work before a heat pump is the right answer. Others with upgraded insulation are well suited. A free heat loss survey gives you a specific answer for your property. If a heat pump is not the right answer yet, we tell you what would need to change before it could be.

How long does a heat pump installation take?

A typical air source heat pump installation takes two to three days. This covers removing the existing boiler or heating system, installing the outdoor unit and indoor cylinder, running any new pipework, commissioning the system, and explaining the controls. Properties that need radiator upgrades across multiple rooms may take an additional day. We give you a clear timeline before the installation starts and keep you updated throughout.

How much is the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant and how do I get it?

The Boiler Upgrade Scheme currently provides £7,500 towards the cost of an air source heat pump installation in England and Wales. From July 2026, properties off the gas grid are eligible for £9,000. The grant is deducted from your installation invoice. You never receive the money directly. To qualify, you need to own the property, be replacing a fossil fuel heating system, and use an MCS-certified installer. The installer handles the BUS application on your behalf. You need a valid EPC for the property. If your EPC has outstanding recommendations for loft or cavity wall insulation, those must be addressed before the BUS voucher can be issued.
