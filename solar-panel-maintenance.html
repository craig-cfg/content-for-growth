My Solar Panels Are Working — So Why Is My Electricity Bill Still So High?

Solar panels that appear to be working do not always reduce your electricity bill at the rate you expect. The most common reason is a mismatch between when the panels generate and when the household uses electricity. Most daytime generation leaves for the grid at export rates unless a battery stores it. Other causes include an inverter that is gradually losing output, panel degradation accelerated by soiling or shade, a system that was poorly specified at installation, or a Smart Export Guarantee tariff you have never registered for. Most of these issues are identified by a thorough annual solar panel maintenance check.

If your monitoring app shows generation and the inverter light is green, your system is working. But working and performing at the level it was designed to are two different things, and the gap between those two is where most of the disappointment hides. Solar panel maintenance covers more than cleaning and a visual inspection. It means verifying that what your system generates is actually reducing what you pay, not what you are exporting to the grid for a few pence a unit while you buy it back in the evening at five times the price.

The five causes below are what an experienced solar engineer checks, in order, when a homeowner calls to say the panels are running but the bill has not moved. If any of them applies to your system, the fix is usually less disruptive than you might expect. If more than one applies, an annual service is the most cost-effective way to find out.

Your panels are generating, but where is that electricity going?

This is where most people's understanding of solar stops: generation. The app shows kilowatt-hours produced, you feel reassured, and then the energy bill arrives and does not match what you expected.

What the app does not show you is self-consumption: the proportion of what your panels generated that your household actually used at the moment of production. In the UK, your electricity supplier does not give you credit on a one-for-one basis for every unit you generate. Units you use directly are worth their full import value (around 27p/kWh at current rates). Units you export earn your Smart Export Guarantee rate, typically somewhere between 4p and 15p/kWh depending on which tariff you have chosen. That gap is the core of the problem for most households whose bills have not shifted the way they expected.

If your household leaves for work at eight in the morning and comes home at six in the evening, your panels spend most of the day generating electricity that nobody is using. That electricity goes to the grid. You receive export payments for it. Then when you cook dinner, run the washing machine, and charge devices in the evening, you buy it all back from the grid at the full import rate. The solar system saved you less than it should have that day, not because it failed, but because the generation and the consumption were several hours apart.

Part of the solution is behavioural: running the dishwasher and washing machine during daylight hours captures more of the day's generation before it reaches the grid. The more significant fix is a battery. A battery stores midday generation and releases it in the evening, shifting the economics considerably. Without a battery, most households self-consume somewhere between 30% and 50% of what their panels generate. With a well-specified battery and a sensible time-of-use tariff, that figure can reach 75% or higher. If your bill has not improved since the panels went in and you do not have battery storage, the timing mismatch is the most likely explanation, and worth addressing before looking at anything else.

The inverter is the first thing we check

The inverter converts the DC electricity your panels produce into the AC electricity your home uses. Without it working at full capacity, nothing the panels generate reaches your appliances at the rate it should.

Inverters have a shorter lifespan than panels. A string inverter, the single unit usually wall-mounted in a garage or utility room, typically lasts between 10 and 15 years. Many systems installed during the Feed-in Tariff era between 2010 and 2019 have inverters that are approaching or past that window. When a string inverter fails completely, it is obvious: a fault code appears, and generation drops to zero or near-zero. That kind of failure you notice immediately.

What homeowners consistently miss is gradual degradation. The inverter continues to operate. It shows no error code. The generation figures look broadly normal unless you compare them month-by-month against the same period from two or three years ago. An inverter that is producing 20% below its rated output does not announce itself. It just quietly costs you money every day.

Hard failure vs gradual degradation: what to look for

A hard inverter failure produces a fault code on the display and generation drops to zero or close to zero, often overnight. You will notice because the app stops reporting generation. This needs diagnosis and usually replacement.

Gradual degradation produces no fault code. Generation appears broadly normal to a casual look at the app, but output is 15% to 30% below the system's design specification. Without comparing logged data across the same months year-on-year, this is invisible to the homeowner. An annual maintenance check reads the performance logs and catches it.

On every maintenance visit we pull the inverter's performance logs and compare DC input against AC output over the past twelve months. If the performance ratio has dropped from where it was at installation, the inverter is underperforming regardless of what the display says. We stock inverters for the brands we install, and we work on systems from other installers too. If your inverter is more than ten years old and your bill has not improved the way you expected, this is the place to start.

Panel degradation is real, but it can be accelerated

Solar panels lose a small amount of output every year. The figure used in standard MCS yield projections is approximately 0.5% per year. A system that has been running for ten years generates roughly 5% less than it did when new. This is expected, it is factored into any reputable performance guarantee, and on its own it does not explain a bill that has barely moved.

What is not expected, and what we find regularly on maintenance visits, is degradation that has been pushed well beyond the normal rate by something external.

Bird fouling is the most common cause. A stripe of droppings across a panel or two, in the wrong place on the cell, can reduce those panels' output by 20% or more. Because most string inverter systems are wired so that underperformance on one panel affects the whole string it belongs to, two fouled panels can pull down the output of four or six. The panels themselves are fine. They are not broken. They need cleaning. But you have to know which panels are affected and where the fouling is sitting before cleaning makes sense. You cannot tell that from standing in the garden.

Shading changes are the second cause. Trees grow. Extensions go up. A neighbour adds a roof structure. A roof that was fully unshaded when the system was surveyed a decade ago may now have afternoon shade falling across part of the array. The performance data will show the impact, but only if someone is reading it systematically rather than glancing at a total figure.

Micro-cracks from rough installation or storm events are rarer, but worth checking on systems more than seven or eight years old. A cracked cell reduces a panel's output and, depending on severity, can create a hot spot that shortens the panel's remaining working life faster than normal degradation would.

Your system may have been designed wrong from day one

This is the part of the conversation most solar content skips. We are not going to skip it.

The cheapest solar quote in 2015 or 2016 often reflected a faster site survey. Panels placed on whichever roof sections were easiest to scaffold and quickest to wire, rather than the sections with the best solar access. Shading analysis done quickly, or not done properly. An inverter sized to what the installer had available rather than what the array actually required. These decisions were made at the moment of installation and are baked into the system's performance for its entire life.

We see the consequences of this regularly. A panel string sitting on a section of roof that receives partial shade for three hours every afternoon. An inverter that was undersized relative to the panel capacity and has been clipping the system's peak output since installation day. A roof where the west-facing section was included in the array when a south-only installation with fewer panels would have produced better results, because the shaded west panels drag down the south-facing ones they share a string with.

A system on an east or west-facing roof generates measurably less than the same system on a south-facing roof under the same conditions. A system on a partially shaded section of roof, regardless of orientation, can generate considerably less still. Neither issue is visible from the app unless someone compares the actual generation data against the expected yield for the system's specific configuration. The European Commission's PVGIS tool is what MCS-certified installers use to calculate expected annual yield by location, orientation, and pitch. That calculated yield is the baseline against which a diagnostic visit compares real-world performance.

If your system has never quite hit the generation figures you were shown at the point of the quote, the original design is worth examining. A diagnostic visit maps what the system is actually producing against what it should be producing given its configuration, and identifies where the gap is. The fix is sometimes smaller than the problem: repositioning, adding optimisers to manage underperforming strings, or adjusting the inverter configuration. If the issues go further, we cover the full range of solar panel repairs on systems of any age and any original installer.

The SEG: are you actually being paid for what you export?

If your system was installed before January 2020, the SEG is not automatic. It requires a separate application.

The Feed-in Tariff worked differently. Eligibility was registered through your installer, and payments were issued automatically based on your generation figures. The Smart Export Guarantee is a separate government-backed scheme that requires each generator to apply directly to a licensed electricity supplier of their choice. Ofgem's Smart Export Guarantee guidance for solar generators sets out the process clearly: you apply to a SEG licensee, they agree a tariff rate above zero, and payments are made based on actual meter readings.

At almost every maintenance visit on a system installed before 2020, we check whether the homeowner is registered. A significant proportion are not. They transitioned off the FiT when it was superseded and have been exporting electricity to the grid for nothing ever since.

If you are registered, the rate you are receiving is worth reviewing. Tariff rates vary between licensees, and you are entitled to change licensee without affecting your electricity supply contract. You do not have to use the same supplier for import and export. One practical requirement: the SEG needs a smart meter. If you do not have one, your supplier will install one for free on request.

The broader point is this. The SEG rate will never match the import rate. Every unit you export earns less than the same unit would save if you had used it at home. Increasing the proportion of your generation you use directly, through shifting consumption into daylight hours or adding battery storage, is almost always more valuable than chasing a better export rate. The SEG is worth having. It is not the answer to a high bill.

What an annual solar panel maintenance visit actually covers

Most of what we have described above is invisible from the ground and invisible to a monitoring app. On a maintenance visit, we look at what the inverter has been doing for the past twelve months, not just what it is doing today. Panel strings that have been underperforming for a year do not look broken on a casual glance at the app. They just log a lower number, and the app does not flag that as a problem. We compare DC input against AC output month by month, identify which panels or strings are under-contributing relative to the array average, check connection integrity and moisture ingress points, verify the monitoring calibration, and review the SEG registration. That sequence is the difference between a system that is working and a system that is working at the level it was designed to.

We service systems we did not install. If your original installer has gone out of business, which applies to a considerable number of companies that were active during the Feed-in Tariff era, that is not a reason to leave the system unchecked. The annual solar panel maintenance service is the right moment to work through all of this properly, before the slow-moving problems above compound into something more expensive.

If the system is more than seven years old, if the bill has not shifted the way you expected, or if your monitoring data shows a dip in output that you cannot account for, now is a sensible time to have it looked at.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my solar panels are generating as much as they should?

Compare your system's generation for the same months across multiple years, not just against today's figure. Most monitoring apps allow you to export historical data. If your generation for a given month is noticeably lower than the same month two years ago and the weather was broadly similar, something has changed. A performance ratio calculation, comparing actual AC output against the DC input your panels are producing, gives a more precise picture. This is one of the checks carried out on an annual maintenance visit, because it requires reading the inverter's internal logs rather than relying on the app's summary figures.

What are the signs that my inverter is starting to fail without a complete breakdown?

Gradual inverter degradation rarely announces itself clearly. The most common indicator is a slow, consistent drop in generation across equivalent months year on year, with no corresponding change in weather patterns or consumption habits. You may also notice the inverter running warmer than it used to, or see small discrepancies between what the inverter display reports and what your electricity meter records. A string inverter that is losing efficiency will often continue to show a green status light until the problem is significant. Checking the performance logs rather than the status indicator is the only reliable way to catch this early.

My system is eight years old and the app still shows good generation. Does it still need a maintenance check?

Yes, and this is exactly the scenario where a maintenance check earns its cost. A system showing good headline generation numbers can still be operating at 20% or 30% below its design specification if an inverter is degrading gradually, if panel fouling is affecting specific strings, or if shading changes have reduced output on part of the array. The app shows generation. A maintenance check shows whether that generation matches what the system should be producing. At eight years old, an inverter is approaching the age at which gradual degradation becomes common, and catching it early gives you more options than waiting until it fails completely.

How often should solar panels be professionally serviced?

Once a year is the standard recommendation for most domestic systems, for the same reason you service a boiler annually rather than waiting for it to break. Solar systems require less frequent intervention than heating systems, but the components that fail slowly, including inverters, connections, and junction box weatherproofing, need someone to look at the data systematically rather than waiting for a visible fault. A visual inspection every year, combined with a performance log review and connection check, keeps the system performing at the level it was designed to and gives early warning of components approaching the end of their working life.

Can you service solar panels that were installed by a different company?

Yes. We work on systems regardless of who installed them, and this includes systems whose original installer is no longer trading. We can diagnose faults, replace inverters, clean panels, check connections, and review performance data on any MCS-compliant system. If the original installer has gone, we can also help you access your monitoring platform, check your MCS certificate registration, and review your SEG status. The only thing we cannot do is take responsibility for installation decisions made before we were involved. But we can tell you clearly what they were and what, if anything, needs addressing.

What is the SEG and how do I know if I am registered for it?

The Smart Export Guarantee is the government-backed scheme that pays solar panel owners for electricity they export to the grid. It replaced the Feed-in Tariff in January 2020, but unlike the FiT it is not automatic. You need to apply directly to a licensed electricity supplier who offers an SEG tariff. To check whether you are registered, log into your electricity supplier's online account and look for an export tariff or SEG contract in your account details. If you cannot find one, contact your supplier directly. If you have a smart meter, the process is straightforward. If you do not, your supplier will install one for free before registering you. The current list of suppliers offering SEG tariffs is published by Ofgem.
