Is a Home Battery Worth It?

For most homes with solar panels, a battery is worth installing. It lets you use the electricity your panels generate instead of exporting it cheaply and buying it back expensively at night. The three things that decide how worthwhile it is are how much solar you generate, how much you use during the day, and what you pay for grid electricity in the evening. Get those three numbers and the answer becomes straightforward.

If you have solar panels and someone has suggested adding a battery, you are probably trying to work out whether the figures actually add up. Most articles about home battery storage either tell you yes without showing you the maths, or hedge so much that you leave none the wiser. We would rather give you the honest version, including the situations where we would tell a customer to hold off.

The Question Most Articles Skip

A battery does one thing. It stores electricity your solar panels generate during the day so you can use it later, usually in the evening when the panels have stopped generating and grid electricity is at its most expensive.

Without a battery, any solar electricity you do not use during the day gets exported to the grid. You get paid for it under the Smart Export Guarantee scheme, which Ofgem requires suppliers to offer. The rate is set by your supplier and must always be above zero, but export rates are typically much lower than the rate you pay to import electricity. So you are selling cheap and buying back expensive. A battery lets you cut out that round trip.

The question is not whether a battery does that. It does. The question is how much money it actually saves you, and whether that saving justifies the cost over the battery's working life.

The Three Things That Actually Determine the Answer

Ignore the headline payback figures. They are averages, and your home is not average. What matters is your specific combination of three things.

How much solar you generate. A 4kWp system in the north of England generates less than the same system in the south because irradiance is lower. A smaller annual generation figure means less surplus to store. It is not a reason not to get a battery, but it does affect the size you need and the savings you can expect.

How much of that solar you use during the day. This is self-consumption, and it is the number most people overlook. If you are at home during the day and running appliances while the panels are generating, your self-consumption is high, and there is less surplus to store. If your house is empty during the day and you use most of your electricity in the evenings, your self-consumption is low and a battery has more to do. Most households fall somewhere in the middle.

What you pay for grid electricity. The higher your evening import rate, the more money you save by using stored solar instead. If you are on a time-of-use tariff with a high evening rate, a battery earns its keep faster. If you are on a flat-rate tariff, the saving per unit is smaller.

These three variables interact. A household with a lot of solar generation, low daytime self-consumption, and a high evening import rate will see the strongest case for a battery. A household with a small system, high daytime self-consumption, and a flat tariff will see a weaker one. We work this out for every customer before recommending a size, because quoting a battery without knowing those numbers is guesswork.

What a Battery Earns Back in the Real World

A few honest figures help here.

The Energy Saving Trust, in its April 2026 battery storage guidance, puts the SEG payment for a typical solar home at around £220 a year. That is the amount you would receive for exporting surplus electricity without a battery. With a battery, you keep more of that electricity and use it yourself rather than exporting it, which saves you the difference between the import rate and the export rate on every unit.

The same Energy Saving Trust guidance puts the cost of a 5kWh battery system at around £4,600. A 13.5kWh unit reflects what a typical household with a 4kWp system actually needs across a full year rather than just a summer evening.

The lifespan of a battery is roughly 10 to 12 years. That is shorter than solar panels, so you should expect to replace the battery before the panels. Factor that into the cost when you are looking at payback calculations.

The honest answer on payback is that it varies. A household with the right combination of generation, consumption pattern, and tariff will see the investment pay back well within the battery's working life. A household at the other end of those variables will take longer, or may find a smaller battery is the right answer rather than none at all.

When We Would and Wouldn't Recommend One

We would suggest a battery if your solar panels are already installed, your daytime self-consumption is low because the house is empty during working hours, and you are on or considering a time-of-use tariff. That is the combination where a battery earns the most.

We would also suggest one if you are installing solar panels now. Fitting the battery at the same time saves on installation costs because the engineer is already on site and the system can be integrated from the start.

We would be more cautious, and we would say so clearly, if your panels are modest in size, your household uses a lot of electricity during the day, or you are already on a flat-rate tariff with no plans to change. In those cases, we would rather have an honest conversation about whether the investment makes sense for your situation than sell you a battery that sits half-empty.

We would also say: an oversized battery is money parked in a box. We size for how you actually use electricity, not for the biggest unit we can fit. That is not a sales angle. It is the thing we see go wrong most often when customers come to us having had a quote from a firm that defaulted to the largest system on the shelf.

The Tariff That Changes the Maths

One thing that has shifted the battery calculation significantly is the growth of smart time-of-use tariffs. These offer cheaper overnight electricity — often between 11pm and 5am — and higher rates at peak times in the evening.

With a battery and a smart tariff, you can charge the battery overnight from the grid at the cheaper rate, and use that stored electricity in the evening when the rate goes up. Combined with solar charging during the day, this gives you two sources of cheap electricity and significantly reduces what you spend at peak times. For the right household, this can change the payback calculation considerably.

The key is a tariff that creates a meaningful spread between the cheap overnight rate and the peak evening rate. A flat tariff reduces that opportunity. If you are considering switching tariff, it is worth asking about export rates as well as import rates — both sides of the equation matter.

Common Questions

Do I need solar panels to get a home battery?

No. You can install a battery without solar panels and use it purely with a smart time-of-use tariff, charging overnight when electricity is cheapest and using the stored power during peak hours. The financial case is typically stronger when paired with solar, because the battery then has two ways to fill itself cheaply. Without solar, the saving comes only from the tariff spread. We can advise on whether a standalone battery makes sense for your home when we do the survey.

How much does home battery storage cost?

The Energy Saving Trust puts a 5kWh system at around £4,600. The right comparison is always cost against the saving it will generate in your specific home. We give a fixed written price before any work begins. There are no figures that creep up after you commit.

How long does a home battery last?

Most home batteries have a working life of around 10 to 12 years, which is shorter than solar panels. You should plan to replace the battery at least once during the lifetime of your solar installation. Battery technology is improving, and the costs of replacement have fallen, but it is a real cost to factor into your calculation. Ask your installer about the warranty on the battery unit specifically, as this is separate from the installation warranty.

Can I get paid for the electricity my battery exports?

Yes. Under the Smart Export Guarantee scheme regulated by Ofgem, electricity suppliers are required to offer a payment for electricity you export to the grid. If your battery is full and your solar panels are still generating, surplus electricity can be exported and you will be paid for it at the SEG rate. The rate varies by supplier and must always be above zero. Shopping around between SEG tariffs is worth doing.

Is home battery storage worth it if I already have solar panels?

It depends on your self-consumption rate, the size of your solar system, and your current tariff. If you export a lot of electricity during the day and then buy it back in the evening, a battery lets you break that cycle and use your own electricity instead. The saving per unit is the difference between your import rate and your export rate. We work this out before recommending a battery, so you know what you are actually getting before you decide.

What size home battery do I need?

Battery size depends on your daily electricity use, how much surplus your solar panels generate, and whether you plan to charge from the grid overnight as well as from solar. A typical household using around 8 to 12 kWh per day will often find a 10 to 15kWh battery the right fit, but that varies. Oversizing is common and wastes money. We size every installation from your actual consumption data and your solar generation figures rather than defaulting to the biggest unit available.
