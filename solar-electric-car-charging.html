Can My Solar Panels Charge My Electric Car — and Does the Maths Actually Work?

Yes, solar panels can charge an electric car, but the answer to whether the maths works depends on how your household uses both. A typical 4kW solar system generates roughly as many kilowatt-hours per year as a typical driver needs to charge their EV. The catch is timing: solar generates during the day and most people charge their car overnight. Without a smart charger or battery, most of your solar will export to the grid rather than reaching your car. With a solar-divert smart charger, surplus daytime generation is redirected to the car. With a battery, daytime solar is stored and released overnight. And in some households with a good SEG export rate and a cheap overnight tariff, the most financially efficient approach is to export your solar and charge the car cheaply from the grid, which earns you more than it costs.

The combination of solar panels and an electric car is one of the most financially compelling things a household can do with its electricity. Used well, the solar panel system offsets the cost of running the car to the point where driving becomes close to free. But the route to getting there is not as simple as plugging the car into the house and assuming the solar panels will fill it up. The timing problem is real, the solution depends on the household, and there are a few counterintuitive moves that a comparison site will not tell you about.

This article works through the maths from first principles and explains what a household with solar panels actually needs in place to make EV charging from solar work.

How much electricity does your electric car actually need?

Start with the car's consumption. Most modern electric cars achieve between 3.5 and 4.5 miles per kilowatt-hour in real-world UK driving. A typical driver covering 10,000 miles per year in a car achieving 4 miles per kilowatt-hour needs approximately 2,500 kilowatt-hours to charge the car over a full year. That is roughly one full 60 to 75 kilowatt-hour battery top-up per week.

At the current standard electricity rate of approximately 24p per kilowatt-hour, charging that car entirely from the grid at full-rate hours costs around £600 per year. On an off-peak EV tariff at 7 to 9p per kilowatt-hour, the same charging costs £175 to £225 per year. The difference between charging at full rate and charging at off-peak rate is already larger than most people expect, and it is the number that makes the solar plus EV equation interesting before a single watt of solar generation enters the picture.

How much does a solar system generate, and what is the timing problem?

A 4kW solar system generates approximately 3,000 to 3,400 kilowatt-hours per year. A typical driver needs around 2,500 kilowatt-hours to charge their car over the same period. On paper, the system generates more than enough to cover the car.

In practice, solar generation and EV charging almost never happen at the same time. Solar panels produce electricity between around 7am and 6pm in summer, and for a shorter window in winter. Most EV owners plug their car in when they get home in the evening and charge overnight. Without something in between, the solar electricity and the car electricity exist in completely separate time windows. The solar exports to the grid during the day. The car charges from the grid at night. The two are financially connected, through the export payment you receive and the import payment you make, but they are not physically connected.

This timing mismatch is the core challenge. Solving it is what the rest of this article is about.

Three approaches: what each one actually does

There are three ways to get solar generation into your electric car, and they have different costs, different efficiencies, and different households they suit best.

The first is a solar-divert smart EV charger. A charger like the Zappi from myenergi monitors your solar generation in real time and redirects any surplus generation to the car before it reaches the export meter. If your panels are generating 3kW and the house is using 1kW, the charger diverts the spare 2kW to the car. When the surplus drops, the charge rate drops. When the panels stop generating, charging stops unless you have set it to top up from the grid. This approach works well for households where the car is at home during working hours, particularly households where someone works from home or uses the car for short local trips. It is much less effective for households where the car leaves early in the morning and comes back at 6pm, by which point the day's generation has already exported.

The second is battery storage. A home battery stores daytime solar surplus and releases it on demand. If the battery is large enough to cover your evening and overnight EV charging, the car effectively runs on stored solar. The calculation from the battery storage article applies here: a 10kWh battery can store a significant portion of a bright day's surplus and release it overnight when the car charges. In summer this is effective. In winter, when a 4kW system may only generate 4 to 8 kilowatt-hours on a cloudy day, the battery will not always fill from solar alone and will draw from the grid to supplement.

The third approach is the one most people do not expect to work best: use the grid as your buffer. Export your solar generation during the day at the Smart Export Guarantee rate, then charge the car overnight at a cheap off-peak EV tariff. The car never directly receives solar electricity, but the household is financially better off because the SEG export rate and the overnight import rate are working in your favour simultaneously.

The counterintuitive maths

If your SEG export rate is 15p per kilowatt-hour and your overnight EV tariff is 7p per kilowatt-hour, then exporting a unit of solar earns 15p and charging the car with a unit of grid electricity costs 7p. You are 8p better off per unit than if you had used the solar directly to charge the car, where the saving would be 24p minus the 15p export you gave up, which nets to 9p per unit. In that scenario, direct solar charging is worth only 1p more than the grid-and-tariff approach, and in households with a high SEG rate the difference disappears or reverses. The most financially efficient approach is not always the most intuitive one.

When direct solar charging is the right choice

The grid-and-tariff approach requires a cheap overnight tariff, which means switching energy supplier or tariff, and it requires the discipline to charge overnight rather than opportunistically during the day. For households where the car is at home all day, or where the occupants prefer not to change tariff, a solar-divert smart charger captures real value directly.

In summer, a car parked at home while its owner works generates a compelling case for solar divert. A typical sunny summer day produces 20 to 30 kilowatt-hours from a 4kW system. If the house is using 4 to 6 kilowatt-hours during the day and the battery is already full or absent, the surplus available to divert to the car can be 10 to 20 kilowatt-hours, enough to add 40 to 80 miles of range at effectively zero cost. The Zappi charger in ECO+ mode handles this automatically.

In winter the case is weaker. A 4kW system may generate 4 to 8 kilowatt-hours on a typical December day, the house may consume most of that itself, and the surplus available for the car is minimal. An overnight cheap tariff does not have this seasonal problem: the overnight rate is the same in December as it is in July.

The best household for a solar-divert smart charger: someone who works from home, has a relatively short daily drive, and parks the car on the drive during daylight hours. The best household for the export-and-reimport approach: someone who commutes, parks at an office, and comes home after generation has stopped for the day.

What you need in place to make it work

If you are installing solar and an EV charger together, the practical requirements are straightforward but worth confirming at the survey stage.

You need an EV charger with solar compatibility if you want the direct divert approach. Not all smart chargers do this. The Zappi from myenergi is the most widely installed solar-compatible charger in the UK and works with all major inverter brands. It requires a CT clamp to monitor generation and load, which the installer fits as part of the installation. Other chargers such as the Ohme and some hybrid-inverter-native chargers offer similar functionality. Confirm the charger model and solar integration method at the quote stage.

You need a smart meter for the overnight tariff approach. Smart meters measure half-hourly import and export, which most time-of-use tariffs require. If you do not already have a smart meter, your energy supplier will install one for free on request.

You need to notify your Distribution Network Operator when combining solar generation and EV charging above certain thresholds. G98 guidance from the Energy Networks Association covers connection requirements for solar installations. An MCS-certified installer handles the G98 notification as part of the solar installation. The EV charger installation is a separate electrical circuit and does not normally require a G98 notification, but if the combined export capacity of the solar system changes, the installer should confirm the notification position.

Finally, the Smart Export Guarantee requires a smart meter and an active application to a SEG licensee. If you want to earn export payments on the solar electricity your car is not using, this needs to be set up at or shortly after the installation is commissioned. Your installer should include this in the handover process.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I charge my electric car for free with solar panels?

You can charge it at effectively zero direct cost during the day if your panels generate more than your house uses and the car is plugged in with a solar-divert smart charger in ECO+ mode. In practice, free charging from solar is most realistic in summer, on bright days, when the car is at home during daylight hours. In winter and on overcast days, generation is lower and the surplus available for the car is reduced. The most financially efficient year-round approach for most drivers combines solar export payments and a cheap overnight charging tariff rather than relying on direct solar charging alone.

How many solar panels do I need to charge an electric car?

A typical EV driver covering 10,000 miles per year needs approximately 2,500 kilowatt-hours of electricity for charging. A standard 4kW solar system generates approximately 3,000 to 3,400 kilowatt-hours per year, which is more than enough in total. The limiting factor is not the number of panels but the timing. Panels generate during the day and charging usually happens overnight. A solar-divert smart charger or home battery is needed to physically connect the generation to the car. The panels themselves do not need to be larger. The system around them needs to be smarter.

What is a solar-divert EV charger and how does it work?

A solar-divert EV charger monitors your solar generation in real time using a current sensor clipped around the supply cable. When the panels generate more than the house is using, the charger redirects the surplus to the car instead of allowing it to export to the grid. The charge rate adjusts automatically as generation fluctuates. If a cloud reduces generation, the charge rate drops. If generation stops, charging pauses. The Zappi charger from myenergi is the most widely installed model in the UK and works in ECO+ mode to charge exclusively from solar surplus, or in ECO mode to supplement from the grid if surplus is insufficient to meet a minimum charge rate.

Is it better to charge my EV directly from solar or use the grid at night?

It depends on your SEG export rate and your overnight tariff. If your SEG rate is 15p per kilowatt-hour and your overnight tariff is 7p, then exporting the solar earns you more than the overnight charging costs you. In that scenario, the grid-and-tariff approach earns a net 8p per kilowatt-hour, while direct solar charging saves you 24p minus the 15p export you give up, which is 9p per unit. The difference is small. If your SEG rate is lower or your overnight tariff is higher, direct solar charging pulls ahead. The right answer depends on your specific rates and how often the car is at home during the day.

Do I need a battery to charge my EV with solar?

No. A solar-divert smart charger charges the car directly from surplus solar generation without a battery, and the grid-and-tariff approach does not require a battery either. A battery improves the picture by storing daytime solar surplus for use during the evening and overnight, which is when both the house and the car need electricity. For households who want their EV charging to be maximally solar-powered across all seasons, a battery that can store enough surplus to cover overnight charging requirements is the most effective solution. But it is not a prerequisite for the combination to work financially.

Can I install an EV charger at the same time as solar panels?

Yes, and there are real advantages to doing both together. The survey covers both the panel placement and the charger circuit routing in one visit. Electrical work costs are shared. The installer can confirm the cable routing from the consumer unit to the charger position at the same time as designing the panel layout and inverter location. A combined installation also ensures the solar monitoring system is integrated with the charger from day one, rather than retrofitting a smart charger to an existing system and having to reconfigure the monitoring.
