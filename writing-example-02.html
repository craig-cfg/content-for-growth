What conservation officers actually look for when you want to replace steel windows

Replacing windows in a conservation area is possible in many cases, and for properties with original steel frames, slimline aluminium is typically the right modern equivalent. Whether you need planning permission depends on whether your area has an Article 4 Direction and whether your property is listed. Conservation officers assess profile accuracy, proportions, sight lines, opening configuration, and colour. They are assessing how the replacement reads, not just what material it is made from.

If your property is in a conservation area and you have original steel windows that need replacing, the question you are probably most anxious about is the one we hear most often: will I be allowed to replace them at all? The answer, in most cases, is yes. But the route to that yes depends on understanding what a conservation officer actually looks at when they assess an application, and what makes the difference between an application that goes through cleanly and one that stalls.

What follows is the process as we actually experience it, not as the guidance documents describe it from a distance. If your property is a 1920s to 1950s house with original Crittall-style steel frames, this article is written specifically for you. Everything here applies to England. Scotland and Wales have different planning frameworks.

First, do you actually need planning permission?

For most houses, replacing windows with similar-looking ones does not automatically require planning permission. Planning Portal's guidance on when planning permission is needed for windows confirms that you do not usually need to apply when inserting new windows that are of a similar appearance to those used in the original construction of the house. The key word is "usually."

Two things change that picture. The first is an Article 4 Direction. This is a decision made by your local council, named after Article 4 of the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) Order, that removes your standard right to replace windows without permission. In plain English: the council has decided that window replacements in this specific area need approval before they go ahead. Many conservation areas have them. Some do not. The only way to know whether yours does is to check your council's planning pages or call the planning department directly. It sounds daunting. It is usually a short conversation.

The second is listed building status. If your property appears on the national register of listed buildings, you will need Listed Building Consent for any significant changes to the windows. This is a separate process from standard planning permission, with a higher bar for approval. Conservation areas and listed building status are not the same thing. Many 1930s houses in conservation areas are not individually listed, which makes the planning picture considerably more straightforward. It is worth checking both before assuming either applies to you.

How to check your property's planning situation

Start with your local council's planning pages and look for an interactive map showing conservation area boundaries. Search for any Article 4 Direction documents covering your specific area. Then check the Historic England list of listed buildings, available at historicengland.org.uk, to see whether your property has individual listed status. A phone call to the planning department takes fifteen minutes and gives you a definitive answer before you spend any money.

What a conservation officer actually looks at

Most homeowners assume that conservation officers refuse any window that is not made from the original material. That is not how the assessment works. Officers are assessing whether the replacement is sympathetic to the character and appearance of the area. For properties with original steel frames, slimline aluminium is the natural like-for-like replacement, and officers in most conservation areas know this product well. The material is not the main question. Five things are.

Profile accuracy. Does the replacement replicate the sight line dimensions and glazing bar depth of the original? The sight line is the visible width of the frame and bars from outside the building. Original Crittall-style steel windows had very narrow sight lines, which is what gave them their character. A replacement with thicker bars reads as wrong from across the road. Conservation officers can see this. It is the most common reason an application fails or comes back with amendment requests.

Proportion. Are the opening sizes, pane divisions, and frame-to-glass ratios consistent with the original? Changing the number of panes, removing glazing bars, or altering the overall shape changes the character of the building. Officers are specifically looking for evidence that the proposed replacement preserves these proportions.

Opening configuration. Does the new window open the same way as the original? A casement that hinged outward should be replaced with a casement that hinges outward, not a tilt-and-turn. Fixed panels should stay fixed. Officers notice when the way a window moves does not match the character of the building's period. We check this at survey stage as a matter of course.

Colour and finish. Is the finish consistent with the period and character of the area? For steel-frame properties, that means black, dark grey, or bronze. Bright or non-traditional colours are not approved. Dual-colour options, where the internal face is a different finish from the external, are generally accepted and do not affect the assessment.

The drawings. Does the application include profile drawings of the proposed replacement at the same scale as the original? This is the practical detail that most applicants miss, and it is the single most common reason applications slow down. We come to this in the next section.

Why slimline aluminium is the right replacement for properties with original steel frames

Much of the conservation area guidance you will find online is written from a timber-first perspective, because most period properties in conservation areas have original timber windows. For properties with original steel frames, that guidance does not apply. The conservation principle is like-for-like: if the original material was steel, the appropriate modern equivalent is not timber. It is slimline aluminium, which replicates the profile and sight line dimensions that define the character of the original.

Historic England's guidance on modifying windows in historic buildings confirms that replacement windows must comply with Approved Document L, which requires new windows to be of the same or better energy efficiency as those being replaced. Slimline double glazing satisfies this. The original Crittall steel window, single-glazed, did not come close to current thermal standards. The slimline aluminium replacement does.

A polyamide thermal break is an insulating barrier set inside the aluminium frame between the inner and outer faces. Without it, the metal frame acts as a direct conductor of heat and cold. With it, the profile performs significantly better and satisfies the thermal requirements without changing the external appearance. This is why we specify a polyamide thermal break as standard on every external window we supply. It is not an optional upgrade. It is what makes the replacement genuinely better than what it is replacing, rather than just better-looking.

For conservation officers, the question when assessing a slimline aluminium application on a steel-frame property is not "why not timber?" It is "does this profile accurately replicate the original?" That is a question we can answer with drawings.

The one thing that makes the biggest difference to your application

Profile drawings. These are technical drawings showing the proposed replacement window in cross-section, at the same scale as the original, including the glazing bar dimensions, frame depth, and profile shape. Conservation officers use them to assess whether the replacement, on paper, reads like the original. An application with a general product specification gives the officer very little to work from. An application with profile drawings gives them exactly what they need to make a decision quickly.

We produce profile drawings for every conservation area project as part of the survey process. The surveyor looks at the original window, records the exact dimensions of the profile, and produces drawings that show our proposed replacement alongside the original. When the drawings accompany the application, the officer can see that the sight lines match, that the glazing bar depth is consistent with the original, and that the replacement will read correctly from outside the building. That is what makes the difference between an application that comes back with questions and one that goes through cleanly.

A concession worth making: for some listed buildings with very high heritage significance, or for properties in conservation areas with particularly strict local policies, even well-drawn applications benefit from a pre-application consultation with the conservation officer before formal submission. We advise whether this applies when we survey the property. We would rather tell you early and add two weeks to the programme than let you submit into a predictable amendment request.

How conservation areas work across different local planning authorities

Conservation area rules in England are set by each local council, and the rules vary between areas, and sometimes between individual conservation areas within the same council boundary. The same property type in one conservation area might need a planning application while a similar property in a neighbouring town might not. There is no single national answer.

Each council publishes its conservation area appraisal documents online. These explain the specific architectural character of the area and what the council considers important to preserve. Reading the appraisal for your specific area gives you a clearer picture of what the planning officer will prioritise than any general guidance can. Look in particular for any mention of window types, materials, or Article 4 Directions. If the appraisal is not clear, a brief call to the planning department settles the question.

If your property is individually listed, the process differs in both route and timescale. Listed Building Consent involves the planning authority's heritage team, and for Grade I and Grade II* listed buildings, Historic England is also a statutory consultee. Most 1920s to 1950s properties in conservation areas are not individually listed. Grade II consent applications for windows, where the case is well-supported and the drawings are thorough, are routinely granted.

What happens when we survey a conservation area property

When we visit a property in a conservation area for an initial survey, we are doing several things at once. The surveyor assesses the condition of the original steel frames and the timber sub-frames that house them. In most 1920s to 1950s properties, those sub-frames — often mature oak — are still structurally sound despite looking tired on the surface. The paint may be in poor condition. The internal beading may need replacing. But the timber underneath is usually fine, and in our experience it has often been there for close to a century without significant deterioration.

Where the timber is sound, we fit the new aluminium window into the existing frame rather than removing the whole assembly. This means less disruption to your building, lower cost, and a better outcome for the conservation case, because retaining the original timber preserves the historic fabric of the structure. Conservation officers notice when a specialist has kept the original sub-frame in place rather than ripping it out. You can read more about how this works on our fitting replacement windows into the existing timber frames page.

The surveyor also records the exact dimensions of the original windows, including profile depth, glazing bar widths, and sight line dimensions. These measurements feed the profile drawings we produce after the survey. At the same time, we assess the planning context: which conservation area the property falls within, whether an Article 4 Direction applies, and whether the property has any listed building status.

From survey to a finished application-ready drawing set takes one to two weeks. The planning application, once submitted, typically takes eight to twelve weeks for a straightforward window replacement in a conservation area. For listed building consent applications the timeline is longer, and where pre-application consultation is advisable, we will tell you at the survey stage and factor it into the programme. Once consent is granted and the order is placed, manufacture and installation typically runs to twelve weeks from the final survey measurement. We work with directly employed installation teams throughout, so the same people involved in the survey and drawing stage are involved through to the final install.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need planning permission to replace windows in a conservation area?

For most houses, you do not automatically need planning permission for a like-for-like window replacement, even in a conservation area. But this changes if your area has an Article 4 Direction, which many conservation areas do. An Article 4 Direction removes your standard permitted development right to replace windows without permission, meaning you do need to apply. Listed buildings always require Listed Building Consent for significant window changes, regardless of Article 4. The first step is to check your council's planning pages for Article 4 Direction documents covering your specific conservation area, and to check whether your property is individually listed on the Historic England register.

Will a conservation officer refuse slimline aluminium windows because the originals were steel?

Unlikely, provided the aluminium profile accurately replicates the sight line dimensions and proportions of the original steel frames. The conservation principle is like-for-like: for properties with original steel windows, slimline aluminium is the natural modern equivalent, and officers in most conservation areas are familiar with it. The material is not the deciding factor. What officers assess is whether the replacement reads like the original from outside the building, which comes down to profile accuracy, glazing bar dimensions, and proportion. A well-drawn application from a specialist who knows what the officer needs to see gives the slimline aluminium proposal a strong basis for approval.

What are profile drawings and do I need them?

Profile drawings are technical drawings showing the proposed replacement window in cross-section at the same scale as the original, including the glazing bar width, frame depth, and profile shape. Conservation officers use them to assess whether the replacement, on paper, reads like what it is replacing. You are not legally required to include them in every application, but applications without them are harder for officers to assess quickly and often prompt requests for more information, which adds weeks to the process. We produce profile drawings for every conservation area project as part of the survey, and they go with the planning submission as standard.

What is an Article 4 Direction?

An Article 4 Direction is a decision made by a local council, named after Article 4 of the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) Order, that removes your standard right to make certain changes to your property without planning permission. In conservation areas, councils use Article 4 Directions to require planning approval for window replacements that would otherwise be permitted development. Whether your property is affected depends on your specific council and conservation area. Your council's planning pages will list any Article 4 Directions in force, or you can call the planning department directly.

Can I replace just one window, or does the whole property need to be done at once?

You can replace individual windows. There is no requirement to replace all windows at once, and many customers start with the frames in worst condition and return for more over subsequent years. In a conservation area, each replacement should closely match the original profile for consistency with the rest of the building. If you are replacing multiple windows in phases, it is worth keeping records of the profile drawings used for the first phase so that subsequent replacements can be made to the same specification.

My house is listed. Does that mean I cannot replace the windows at all?

Not necessarily. Listed Building Consent is required for any significant changes to a listed building's windows, including replacement, but consent can be granted. The process involves the local planning authority's heritage team and, for higher-graded listed buildings, Historic England as a statutory consultee. The threshold for approval is higher than for a standard conservation area application: the replacement needs to closely replicate the original in proportion, profile, and detail, and the case for replacement needs to be clearly made. A specialist who can produce detailed profile drawings and engage directly with the conservation officer's requirements gives a listed building application a much stronger basis than a general product specification alone.
