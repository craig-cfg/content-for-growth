How Manchester's irradiance compares to the rest of England

Solar generation is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh). To estimate how much a system will generate, installers use irradiance figures: the amount of solar energy reaching a square metre of surface per year, measured in kWh per kilowatt-peak (kWh/kWp). The higher the irradiance, the more electricity a given system produces.

Greater Manchester registers approximately 850 to 950 kWh/kWp annually, depending on exact location and roof orientation. That is the figure from the European Commission's PVGIS irradiance data for North West England, the same tool MCS-certified installers are required to use when producing generation estimates under the MIS 3002 methodology. Brighton registers over 1,100 kWh/kWp. The south coast of England pulls UK-wide averages upward. A comparison site that quotes "typically 3,400 to 4,200 kWh per year" for a 4 kW system is using those averages, not the North West figure.

At 950 kWh/kWp and a performance ratio of approximately 0.82 (which accounts for inverter losses, cable losses, and real-world degradation), a 4 kW south-facing system in Greater Manchester generates around 3,100 to 3,300 kWh per year. That is enough to cover a meaningful share of a typical household's electricity consumption. The average UK home uses approximately 3,400 kWh annually. It is roughly 14% below the south coast figure. It is not 40% below. Manchester is not a marginal solar location. It is a location where honest figures are fourteen percent lower than the national comparison sites suggest, and buyers deserve to know that before they sign anything.

Why solar panels generate on cloudy days: the physics

The question we hear most often before a buyer makes contact is this: "Do solar panels actually work in Manchester weather?" The short answer is yes. The longer answer is more useful.

Solar panels generate electricity from photons, particles of light, not from heat or direct sunshine. Photovoltaic cells respond to the visible light spectrum. On a clear day, most of that light arrives as direct beam radiation in a straight line from the sun. On a cloudy day, it arrives as diffuse radiation: sunlight scattered in all directions by cloud cover, atmospheric moisture, and particles in the air. The sky on an overcast day is still bright. You can read outside, drive without headlights, and take a photograph. That ambient brightness is diffuse light. Your panels capture it.

On a fully overcast day, a 4 kW system in Greater Manchester will typically produce between 10 and 25% of its rated output, roughly 4 to 10 kWh. On a partly cloudy day, output is typically 50 to 80% of a clear-sky day. These are industry-standard published estimates, consistent across multiple published studies on photovoltaic performance in diffuse light conditions. Germany, which has significantly lower annual irradiance than North West England and is one of the cloudiest major economies in Europe, remains one of the world's largest solar producers. The cloudy-day objection is understandable. It is also not a reason to dismiss solar in Manchester.

The more important point is this: the annual generation figure is already the right metric. The 3,100 to 3,300 kWh annual output for a Greater Manchester system is not what your panels generate if the sky is blue every day. It is what they generate averaged across every grey January, every wet April, and every clear August. The PVGIS tool models actual weather data for the North West over a multi-year period. Every overcast day is already in that number.

Roof orientation and angle: what matters for a Greater Manchester home

Greater Manchester sits at approximately 53 degrees north latitude. At that latitude, the sun traces a southern arc across the sky year-round. Panels that face south, and are tilted at roughly 30 to 40 degrees from horizontal, maximise exposure to that arc across all twelve months. That is the theoretical optimum. In practice, most buyers are working with a fixed roof pitch and a fixed orientation, neither of which they can change.

The good news is that deviation from the optimum costs less than most buyers expect. A roof facing southeast or southwest loses only 3 to 5% of annual output compared to due south. A 4 kW system that would generate 3,200 kWh facing south will generate approximately 3,000 to 3,100 kWh facing southeast. A roof facing due east or west produces around 80% of south-facing output, about 2,500 to 2,650 kWh per year for a 4 kW system in Greater Manchester. That is still a meaningful amount of electricity. With battery storage, an east or west-facing system can perform well because it captures morning or afternoon generation and stores it for evening use.

The typical Greater Manchester terraced and semi-detached housing stock, much of it Victorian and Edwardian, two-slope roofs at standard UK pitch of 30 to 40 degrees, sits naturally within the recommended tilt range. Frame adjustment is rarely necessary. What matters more than achieving a precise angle is shading. A chimney at the south end of a terrace, a mature tree behind the rear elevation, or a neighbouring extension that casts a shadow across part of the roof in the afternoon. These reduce output more significantly than a few degrees of deviation from the optimum pitch. The site survey before any installation is where the actual shading profile gets mapped across the year, not estimated from a Google Maps satellite view.

What honest payback looks like in Greater Manchester

Payback calculations for solar depend on three things: how much the system generates, how much of that generation the household uses directly rather than exporting, and what the household pays for electricity. Change any one of those variables and the payback period changes. Comparison sites and national installers often quote payback periods that assume southern irradiance, high self-consumption, and a tariff rate that may not match a specific buyer's situation.

Starting with generation: a 4 kW south-facing system in Greater Manchester typically produces 3,100 to 3,300 kWh per year. At the Ofgem Q1 2026 price cap rate of 27.69p/kWh, every kWh of that electricity you use directly rather than importing from the grid is worth approximately 27.69 pence. A household that self-consumes 35% of generation without battery storage (a realistic figure for a household with working adults out during the day) avoids importing roughly 1,085 to 1,155 kWh of grid electricity per year. The remaining generation is exported via the Smart Export Guarantee. Buyers should check Ofgem's Smart Export Guarantee page for current rates, which change quarterly and are not fixed. Adding battery storage alongside your solar panels typically raises self-consumption to 60 to 80%, which changes the financial case substantially.

Honest payback ranges for a Greater Manchester installation: without battery, ten to fourteen years for a typical 3-bed semi. With battery and a time-of-use tariff that charges the battery cheaply overnight, eight to eleven years is realistic. These are North West figures. They are not the six to seven year paybacks quoted by comparison sites using southern irradiance baselines and optimistic self-consumption assumptions. The difference in irradiance between Manchester and Cornwall is roughly 15 to 20%. Over a 25-year panel lifespan, that matters. It does not make Greater Manchester a bad solar market. It makes accurate local forecasting more important.

One figure worth noting on installation cost: 0% VAT applies to residential solar panel installations until 31 March 2027. That 0% rate applies to the full system cost including panels, inverter, battery (where included), mounting, and installation labour.

A note on savings claims

If an installer or comparison site quotes "save up to X%" without specifying the system size, roof orientation, self-consumption rate, and tariff assumption behind that figure, the number is not meaningful for your specific property. Ask for the kWh generation estimate, the assumed self-consumption percentage, and the electricity rate used. Then apply your own bill to those numbers. A 4 kW system in Whitefield on a north-south-split semi generates a different number from a 6 kW system in Prestwich on a south-facing detached. The site survey is what produces a figure that actually applies to you.

Do solar panels work in winter in Manchester?

Yes, but output drops substantially in December and January due to shorter days and a lower sun angle. A 4 kW system in Greater Manchester typically generates around 100 to 200 kWh across the two darkest months combined, compared to 400 to 500 kWh in July and August. Winter output is already factored into the annual 3,100 to 3,300 kWh figure. The annual total is the right metric for assessing whether solar makes financial sense, not any individual month. Battery storage helps households make better use of summer surplus, but it does not change winter generation.

How much electricity will solar panels generate on a typical cloudy Manchester day?

On a fully overcast day, a 4 kW system in Greater Manchester typically generates 4 to 10 kWh, roughly 10 to 25% of what the same system produces on a clear summer day. On a partly cloudy day, output is 50 to 80% of a clear-sky day. These are not edge cases. They are baked into the annual generation figure. The 3,100 to 3,300 kWh annual total is calculated from PVGIS irradiance data that reflects actual North West weather patterns, including every grey day the region receives. A clear day is not the assumption behind the annual figure. It is one input among many.

Does it matter which direction my roof faces for solar panels in Greater Manchester?

Yes, but less than most buyers expect. South-facing is optimal. Southeast and southwest lose only 3 to 5% of annual output compared to due south. A system facing southwest in Bury will generate nearly as much as one facing due south. East and west-facing roofs produce approximately 80% of south-facing output, which is still viable, particularly with battery storage. What matters more than a few degrees of deviation is shading. A south-facing roof with a chimney blocking the afternoon sun will underperform a southwest-facing roof with no shading. A site survey maps the actual shading profile, which is where the real variables are.

What is the best angle for solar panels on a Greater Manchester roof?

For Greater Manchester at approximately 53 degrees north latitude, the optimal fixed tilt is 30 to 40 degrees from horizontal. Most UK residential roofs sit naturally in this range, which means frame adjustment is rarely required or cost-effective. A standard 35-degree pitch facing south produces close to maximum annual output for this latitude. The more important variable is shading. Panels installed at the precise optimal angle but partially shaded by a chimney or dormer will underperform panels at a slightly sub-optimal angle on a clean, unobstructed roof face.
