Do Solar Panels Work on Cloudy Days? What UK Homeowners Need to Know

The short answer

Yes — solar panels generate electricity on cloudy days by converting diffuse light, the sunlight scattered through cloud cover, not just direct sunshine. On a heavily overcast day, a typical UK system produces around 10 to 25% of its peak output. On a lightly overcast day, that rises to 60 to 80%. Over a full year, a well-sited 4 kWp south-facing system in southern England typically generates 3,400 to 3,700 kWh — enough to make a real dent in your electricity bills, grey days included.

The cloudy days question is the one we hear most often before a homeowner commits to a survey. It's a completely fair thing to ask. If you're spending several thousand pounds on a system that will sit on your roof for decades, you want to know what it does on a grey Tuesday in October — not just a clear day in June.

The answer is more specific than "yes, they still work" and more reassuring than most people expect. This article focuses on cloud cover specifically: what your system produces in different conditions, why the annual generation figure matters more than any single grey day, and what the numbers actually look like for homes in the UK.

How solar panels generate power in low light

Photovoltaic cells generate electricity through the photovoltaic effect: incoming light particles called photons knock electrons loose in the silicon cell material, and those electrons flow through the circuit as electrical current. The key point is that this process responds to photons from any light — including the diffuse light scattered by cloud cover. It's not limited to direct, unobstructed sunshine. A solar panel generating electricity on a grey day is doing exactly what it was designed to do. Cloud is a constraint, not a blocker.

When cloud cover is present, sunlight doesn't disappear — it scatters through the cloud layer and reaches the panel surface from multiple directions at once. This is called diffuse irradiance. Most modern domestic solar panels use monocrystalline silicon cells, which are specifically efficient at converting diffuse irradiance into electricity. Monocrystalline panels consistently outperform older polycrystalline designs in low-light conditions — the performance gap is real, particularly in autumn and winter.

What this means in practice

Panels don't need direct sunshine to generate power. Daylight and direct sunlight are different things. Even on a uniformly grey day in November, the panels are converting the available diffuse irradiance into electricity. The question is how much — and that depends on how dense the cloud cover is.

There's also what engineers call the edge-of-cloud effect. When direct sunlight and reflected light from cloud edges reach a panel at the same moment, output can temporarily exceed the panel's standard test rating. You might see this as a brief spike in your monitoring app. It's real, but brief and unpredictable — don't design around it, and don't let anyone use it to inflate a generation estimate.

What output to expect: from heavy overcast to bright overcast

The 10 to 25% figure that appears in most articles on this subject describes heavily overcast conditions — a thick, unbroken cloud layer with no direct irradiance reaching the panels. But UK cloud cover covers a wide spectrum, and most overcast days are nowhere near the worst case.

A bright overcast day in spring, where the sun is diffused but clearly present, typically produces 60 to 80% of clear-sky peak. A moderately cloudy September afternoon sits somewhere in the middle. The table below gives a practical guide to what to expect across typical UK cloud conditions.

Cloud condition Approximate output (% of clear-sky peak)
Clear sunny day90–100%
Bright partial cloud70–90%
Light uniform overcast40–70%
Moderate overcast25–50%
Heavy overcast10–25%
Thick cloud with heavy rain5–15%

Output ranges based on silicon PV performance characteristics in diffuse-irradiance conditions.

What the table is telling you

The thick, grey, wet days of a British winter do represent a real drop in output. That's not a problem with the panels — it's a reason to design the system properly. A well-specified installation includes battery storage sized to your typical evening consumption, so that on low-generation days, a properly sized battery covers the gap using energy captured on brighter days earlier in the week.

The right question: annual generation, not daily output

Focusing on what the panels produce on a single cloudy day is understandable, but it's the wrong measure for deciding whether solar makes financial sense. The relevant figure is annual generation — how many kilowatt-hours the system produces across a full year, accounting for summer peaks, autumn shoulders, and winter troughs together. A single grey day tells you very little about whether solar delivers value over 25 years.

According to DESNZ solar PV deployment statistics, the UK had more than 21.5 GW of solar capacity installed by the end of 2025, with over 22,000 new residential installations added every month throughout the year. That scale of uptake doesn't happen in a country where solar doesn't work. The production lost in winter is substantially offset by the summer surplus.

Germany is the clearest international comparator. Germany has more solar capacity per capita than almost any other country in the world, despite receiving average annual irradiance broadly comparable to the UK — and in some regions, lower. The financial case for solar in both countries rests on annual generation and electricity prices, not on sunny days alone. A grey week in February doesn't change a year's worth of bill savings.

What battery storage changes about the calculation

Battery storage changes the framing of the cloudy days question entirely. Without a battery, the question is what your system generates today and whether you can use it right now. With a battery, the question becomes what your system has accumulated across the past several days and whether the battery holds enough to cover tonight. That's a fundamentally better position to be in — and it's why we recommend battery storage as part of the standard design rather than an optional extra.

On a run of grey days — the kind of overcast weather that settles in for a week in November — a system without a battery exports most of its reduced output to the grid at a low Smart Export Guarantee rate, then buys expensive grid electricity back in the evening. A system with battery storage accumulates what the panels produce throughout the day, even at reduced rates, and uses it to cover your evening demand. The gap between a good system and a partially designed one is often the battery, not the panels.

How to think about battery sizing

Most buyers focus on the wrong number. A battery sized to your worst-case peak demand can sit largely unused for most of the year — an expensive way to store electricity. The right size is based on your typical evening consumption: what your household actually draws from the grid on a normal evening, accounting for appliances, lighting, and whether an EV charges overnight. For most homes, that figure sits between 5 and 10 kWh. A battery in that range covers the evening comfortably on most overcast days, drawing on whatever the panels generated during daylight hours.

What the local generation numbers show

The South East of England receives more annual solar irradiance than most other UK regions. Global horizontal irradiance for the South East sits at approximately 1,100 to 1,200 kWh per square metre annually, based on PVGIS data from the European Commission's Joint Research Centre. This compares well against the UK national average of roughly 900 kWh per square metre, and northern England and Scotland where the figure drops further.

For a 4 kWp south-facing system installed at a 35-degree pitch in the South East, PVGIS modelling typically produces an annual generation estimate of 3,400 to 3,700 kWh. South Coast sites record some of the highest kWh-per-kWp figures for residential installations in England.

The seasonal picture — be clear-eyed about this

Around 65 to 70% of a system's annual generation falls in the spring and summer months (March through August), with 30 to 35% in the autumn and winter half. December and January are the lowest-output months by a significant margin. That's real and predictable, and no honest installer should smooth over it. It's also the reason the annual figure is the right benchmark — August's surplus builds the case that December cannot make on its own.

What this means for you as a homeowner: the question isn't whether your panels generate on a cloudy day. They will. The question is what the system produces across a year and how that matches your household's electricity use. That's what a proper site survey answers — taking your specific roof, orientation, pitch, and shading into account to produce a generation estimate that reflects your actual property, not a regional average.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do solar panels generate on a heavily overcast day in the UK?

On a heavily overcast day with thick, unbroken cloud cover, a typical UK solar system produces around 10 to 25% of its rated peak output. On a lightly overcast day — which covers a much larger proportion of UK weather — output can stay at 40 to 70% of peak. Individual cloudy days matter, but annual generation is the right measure of whether solar delivers value for your home.

Are solar panels worth getting in the UK given the weather?

Yes. A 4 kWp south-facing system in southern England typically generates 3,400 to 3,700 kWh per year — enough to cover a meaningful share of a household's electricity use. The South East receives around 1,100 to 1,200 kWh per square metre of annual solar irradiance, higher than the UK average and comparable to northern Germany, where solar is one of the most widely deployed energy sources in the world. The financial case is built on annual output, not sunny days.

What is the edge-of-cloud effect and does it help UK solar performance?

The edge-of-cloud effect happens when direct sunlight and light reflected from cloud edges reach a panel at the same time, temporarily pushing output above its rated capacity. It's real, but brief and unpredictable — you might see a spike in your monitoring app. Its contribution to annual generation is marginal and shouldn't factor into any generation estimate.

Do solar panels work in winter in the UK?

Yes. Winter output is lower — around 25 to 30% of peak summer daily output in December and January — but consistent and real. Shorter days and a lower sun angle are the main factors, not cold temperatures. PV cells actually run slightly more efficiently in cool conditions. The winter half of the year (September to February) contributes roughly 30 to 35% of a system's annual generation.

How does battery storage help on cloudy days?

A battery shifts the question from "what did the panels generate today?" to "what has the system stored over the past few days?" On grey days, the panels still generate at reduced output. Without a battery, most of that goes to the grid at a low Smart Export Guarantee rate, then you buy it back in the evening at full price. With a battery, the day's generation accumulates and covers your evening demand. For most homes, a 5 to 10 kWh battery is the right size for this job.
