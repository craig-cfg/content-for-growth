Do Solar Panels Work on Cloudy Days? What UK Homeowners Need to Know

Solar panels generate electricity on cloudy days by converting diffuse light, meaning sunlight scattered through cloud cover, rather than requiring direct sunshine. On heavily overcast days, a typical UK photovoltaic system produces around 10 to 25 percent of its clear-sky peak output. On lightly overcast days, output can remain at 60 to 80 percent of peak. Across a full year, a well-sited 4 kWp south-facing system in southern England typically generates 3,400 to 3,700 kWh, enough to make a meaningful reduction in household electricity costs regardless of how many grey days the year contains.

The cloudy days question is the one we hear most often before a homeowner commits to a survey. It is a fair thing to ask. If you are going to spend several thousand pounds on a system that sits on your roof for decades, you want to know what it actually does on a grey Tuesday in October, not just a clear day in June. The answer is more specific than "yes, they still work" and more reassuring than most people expect. If you want to understand how solar panels work in general, that is covered elsewhere. This article focuses on cloud cover specifically: what your system produces in different conditions, why the annual generation figure matters more than any single grey day, and what the numbers actually look like for homes.

How Solar PV Cells Generate Power in Low Light

Photovoltaic cells generate electricity through the photovoltaic effect: incoming light particles called photons knock electrons loose in the silicon cell material, and those electrons flow through the circuit as electrical current. The key point is that this process responds to photons from any light source, including diffuse light scattered by cloud cover. It is not limited to direct, unobstructed sunlight. A solar panel generating electricity on a grey day is doing exactly what it was designed to do. Cloud is a constraint, not a blocker.

When cloud cover is present, sunlight does not disappear. It scatters through the cloud layer and reaches the panel surface from multiple directions at once. This is called diffuse irradiance. Most modern domestic solar panels use monocrystalline silicon cells, which are specifically efficient at converting diffuse irradiance into electricity. Monocrystalline panels generally outperform older polycrystalline designs in low-light conditions, which is one reason we specify monocrystalline as standard. We have seen the performance difference across installations and the gap is real, particularly in autumn and winter.

There is also what engineers call the edge-of-cloud effect. When direct sunlight and the reflected light from cloud edges reach a panel at the same moment, output can temporarily exceed the panel's standard test rating. You may occasionally see this as a brief spike in your monitoring app. It is real, but it is brief and unpredictable. Do not design around it and do not let anyone use it to inflate a generation estimate.

What this means in practice

Panels never require direct sunshine to generate power. Daylight and direct sunlight are different things. Even on a uniformly grey day in November, the panels are converting the available diffuse irradiance into electricity. The question is how much, and that depends on how dense the cloud cover is.

What Output to Expect From Heavy Overcast to Bright Overcast

The 10 to 25 percent figure that appears in most articles on this subject describes heavily overcast conditions: a thick, unbroken cloud layer with no direct irradiance reaching the panels. Under the heaviest cloud cover, silicon PV output can fall well below that range. As the Energy Saving Trust's guidance on solar panels makes clear, the stronger the sunshine, the more electricity is generated — which means extreme overcast with no direct irradiance can reduce output substantially beyond the typical cloudy-day figure. The 10 to 25 percent range applies to heavy but not extreme overcast.

The practical picture is more varied than a single figure suggests. UK cloud cover covers a wide spectrum, and most overcast days are not the worst case. A bright overcast day in spring, where the sun is diffused but clearly present, typically produces 60 to 80 percent of clear-sky peak. A moderately cloudy September afternoon sits somewhere in the middle. The table below gives a practical guide to what to expect across typical UK cloud conditions.

Cloud condition Approximate output as % of clear-sky peak
Clear sunny day 90 to 100%
Bright partial cloud 70 to 90%
Light uniform overcast 40 to 70%
Moderate overcast 25 to 50%
Heavy overcast 10 to 25%
Thick cloud with heavy rain 5 to 15%

Source: output ranges based on silicon PV performance characteristics in diffuse-irradiance conditions.

We will be honest about what this table shows. The thick, grey, wet days of a British winter do represent a real and significant drop in output. That is not a problem with the panels. It is a reason to design the system properly. A well-specified system includes battery storage sized to the household's typical evening consumption, not just the panel array. On the days the panels generate very little, a properly sized battery covers the gap using energy captured on brighter days earlier in the week. If your system was sold to you as "just panels," the answer to the cloudy days question is also a reason to ask about battery storage.

The Right Question: Annual Generation, Not Daily Output

Focusing on what the panels produce on a single cloudy day is understandable, but it is the wrong measure for deciding whether solar makes financial sense for your home. The relevant figure is annual generation: how many kilowatt-hours the system produces across a full year, accounting for summer peaks, autumn shoulders, and winter troughs together. A single grey day tells you very little about whether solar delivers value over 25 years.

According to DESNZ solar photovoltaics deployment statistics, the UK had more than 21.5 GW of solar PV capacity installed by the end of November 2025, with over 22,000 new residential installations added every month throughout 2025. That scale of uptake does not happen in a country where solar does not work. The production lost in winter months is substantially offset by the summer surplus.

Data from the DESNZ Energy Trends December 2025 statistical release confirms that solar PV's share of UK electricity generation reached 10 percent in Q3 2025, up from 8 percent a year earlier. That share is driven by increased capacity, but the underlying output, measured across all weather conditions, demonstrates that UK irradiance is sufficient to support large-scale, economically meaningful solar generation.

Germany is the clearest international comparator. Germany has more solar capacity per capita than almost any other country in the world, despite receiving average annual irradiance broadly comparable to the UK and in some regions lower. The financial case for solar in both countries rests on annual generation and electricity prices, not on sunny days alone. A grey week in February does not change a year's worth of bill savings.

What Battery Storage Changes About the Calculation

Battery storage changes the framing of the cloudy days question entirely. Without a battery, the question is what your system generates today and whether you can use it directly. With a battery, the question becomes what your system has accumulated across the past several days and whether the battery holds enough to cover tonight's usage. That is a fundamentally better position to be in. It is why we recommend battery storage as part of the standard design rather than as an optional extra.

On a run of grey days, the kind of overcast weather that settles over southern England for a week in November, a system without battery storage exports most of its reduced output to the grid at a low Smart Export Guarantee rate and then imports expensive grid electricity in the evening. A system with battery storage accumulates what the panels produce throughout the day, even at reduced rates, and uses it to cover your evening demand. The gap between a good system and a partially designed one is often the battery, not the panels.

Sizing matters, and most buyers focus on the wrong number. A battery sized to a household's worst-case peak demand can sit largely unused for most of the year. That is an expensive way to store electricity. The right specification is based on typical evening consumption: what your household actually draws from the grid on a normal evening, accounting for appliances, lighting, and whether an EV charges overnight. For most homes, that figure sits between 5 and 10 kWh. A battery in that range covers the evening comfortably on most overcast days, drawing on whatever the panels generated during daylight hours.

What the Local Generation Numbers Show

The South East of England receives more annual solar irradiance than most other regions of the UK. Global horizontal irradiance for the South East, the measure of how much solar energy reaches a horizontal surface per year, sits at approximately 1,100 to 1,200 kWh per square metre annually, based on PVGIS irradiance data from the European Commission's Joint Research Centre. This compares favourably to the UK national average of roughly 900 kWh per square metre at the geographic centre of the country, and to northern England and Scotland where the figure drops further. You are in one of the better parts of the country for solar, even on a cloudy island.

For a 4 kWp south-facing system installed at a 35-degree pitch in the South East, PVGIS modelling typically produces an annual generation estimate of 3,400 to 3,700 kWh. Irradiance benchmarks aligned with MCS certification standards confirm that the South East consistently outperforms the UK national average for annual solar yield, with South Coast sites recording some of the highest kWh-per-kWp figures for residential installations in England. We design every system we install using that location-specific data, not a national average.

The seasonal distribution is important to be clear-eyed about. Roughly 65 to 70 percent of a system's annual generation falls in the spring and summer months (March through August), with the remaining 30 to 35 percent in the autumn and winter half. December and January are the lowest-output months by a significant margin. Shorter daylight hours, a lower sun angle, and more frequent heavy cloud combine to reduce daily generation substantially. That is real and predictable, and no honest installer should smooth over it. It is also the reason the annual figure is the right benchmark. August's surplus builds the case that December cannot make on its own, and a properly sized battery means you are drawing on that stored summer surplus through the winter evenings.

What this means for you as a homeowner is that the question is not whether the panels generate on a cloudy day. They will. The question is what the system produces across a year and how that matches your household's electricity use. That is a question a proper site survey answers. The survey takes your specific roof, orientation, pitch, and any shading into account to produce a generation estimate that reflects the actual property, not a regional average. Frequently Asked Questions

How much do solar panels generate on a heavily overcast day in the UK?

On a heavily overcast day with thick, unbroken cloud cover and no direct solar irradiance, a typical UK solar panel system generates around 10 to 25 percent of its rated peak output. The exact figure depends on cloud density, panel type, and roof orientation. Monocrystalline silicon panels, the standard specification for UK residential installations, perform relatively well in diffuse light compared to older polycrystalline designs. On a lightly overcast day, which represents a larger proportion of UK cloud conditions, output can remain at 40 to 70 percent of peak. Individual cloudy days are a real factor, but annual generation rather than any single grey day is the correct measure of whether solar delivers value for a given property.

Are solar panels worth getting in the UK given the weather?

Yes. The annual generation figures for southern England, approximately 3,400 to 3,700 kWh per year for a 4 kWp south-facing system in the South East, are sufficient to cover a meaningful proportion of a typical household's electricity consumption. The South East receives around 1,100 to 1,200 kWh per square metre of annual solar irradiance, which is higher than the UK national average and broadly comparable to northern Germany, where solar deployment is among the highest in the world. The financial case depends on annual output and electricity prices, not on individual sunny days. A battery storage system, sized to typical evening consumption, strengthens the case further by reducing reliance on grid imports during the evenings and grey periods.

What is the edge-of-cloud effect and does it help UK solar performance?

The edge-of-cloud effect occurs when direct sunlight and the light reflected from cloud edges reach a solar panel simultaneously. The combined irradiance can temporarily push output above the panel's standard test rating, since the panel is receiving more total light energy than under clear-sky conditions alone. This is a genuine physical phenomenon. However, it is brief, unpredictable, and not something to factor into generation estimates or system sizing decisions. It is one reason why solar output monitoring sometimes shows brief spikes above rated capacity on partially cloudy days, but its contribution to annual generation is marginal.

Do solar panels work in winter in the UK?

Yes, solar panels generate electricity in winter, though at significantly lower daily output than in summer. The reduction in winter performance is driven primarily by shorter daylight hours and a lower sun angle, not by cold temperatures. Photovoltaic cells actually operate slightly more efficiently in cool conditions than in high summer heat, because electrical resistance in the cell material decreases as temperature falls. In December and January, a system typically generates around 25 to 30 percent of its peak summer daily output. Across the winter half of the year (September through February), a system produces roughly 30 to 35 percent of its annual generation total. Winter output is real and consistent. It is simply lower than the summer months.

How does battery storage help on cloudy days?

Battery storage shifts the question from what the panels generate on any given cloudy day to what the system has accumulated over the past several days. On grey days, the panels still generate electricity at reduced output. Without a battery, much of that generation is exported to the grid at the Smart Export Guarantee rate and replaced in the evening with more expensive grid imports. With a battery sized to typical evening consumption, the reduced generation from cloudy days accumulates during daylight hours and covers evening demand, reducing grid reliance. The key is sizing: a battery specified to typical evening use rather than maximum load delivers the best return, and for most homes, that means a battery in the 5 to 10 kWh range.
