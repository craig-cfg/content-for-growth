Why Oak Frames Crack, Shake and Move

Green oak is freshly cut timber with a moisture content typically between 60 and 80 percent. As it dries inside a raised frame, it shrinks across the grain and develops surface cracks called checks and deeper splits called shakes. These are not structural defects. The grading rules for structural green oak limit the grain angle so that cracks run along the grain, not across it, and do not reduce load capacity. Properly designed joints are intended to tighten as the wood moves. A frame that cracks and settles in its first three years is behaving correctly.

The first crack in a new green oak frame appears quietly, usually in the first summer after the building is raised. A split along a post face, maybe a hand's width long. It can look alarming if you do not know what you are looking at. We have taken plenty of calls from owners standing in their new building wondering if something has gone wrong. Nothing has. What you are seeing is the frame becoming itself.

This article explains what is actually happening inside the timber, and why the movement of the first years is not a problem to manage but a process to understand. It covers what this timber is and how it is graded, what the different types of visible movement mean, the joinery mechanism that makes the joints stronger as the wood dries, and what a normal timeline looks like from raise day through to a settled frame.

What green oak actually means, and why it is the right structural choice

The word "green" has nothing to do with colour. It refers to the freshness of the timber. Green oak is oak that has been recently felled and has not been seasoned or dried. It carries a high moisture content, typically somewhere between 60 and 80 percent by weight. A freshly raised frame holds a significant amount of water inside its cells, and it will spend the next several years releasing it.

This might seem like a disadvantage. In fact, it is the reason the structural framing industry uses green oak rather than seasoned alternatives. At the section sizes used in a structural frame — posts, tie beams, and braces that may be 150mm, 200mm, or larger across — kiln-drying cannot economically reduce the core moisture content. Kilns work by heating the timber from the outside in. For small joinery sections this is practical. For large structural beams, the outer face dries and hardens before the core has released its moisture, which can cause distortion and damage to the wood's internal structure. The section sizes that make an oak frame strong are too thick to kiln-dry without risk.

Air-drying is the alternative, but it takes time. Large structural oak sections can take many years to air-dry to a stable moisture content. The storage costs, the loss of usable timber to distortion during drying, and the difficulty of sourcing the right sizes at the right moment make air-dried structural oak three or four times the cost at the section sizes used in a frame. And it offers no structural advantage.

There is a third reason green oak is used: it is workable. At its fresh moisture content, the timber can be cut and shaped with hand tools and chisels at the section sizes needed for structural joinery. Seasoned oak at these section sizes is dense enough to blunt tools quickly and makes cutting the precise joints that hold a frame together far harder. The joinery that defines a quality oak frame — the mortice-and-tenon joints, the dovetail scarfs, the wedge compound stop splayed scarf joints — requires a material you can work accurately. Oak in its green state allows this. Fully dried oak at large sections does not.

The oak used in structural framing is TH1/D30 grade. TH1/D30 is the quality grade for structural green oak that specifies the maximum allowable grain angle and limits on knots and other natural features. It is the standard used in structural timber framing. As English Woodlands Timber's guidance on green oak moisture movement notes, moisture movement in structural sections is a design factor that careful specification and good timber detailing can fully account for. A well-specified frame of this kind is designed for what the timber will do, not despite it.

Checks and shakes: what they are and what they are not

If you have been asking why does oak crack, or why does an oak frame shake and move after it is raised, the short answer is: because it is meant to.

When people talk about oak "cracking," they are usually describing one of two things. A check is a surface crack that forms as the outer face of a beam dries faster than the core. The outside contracts, the inside has not yet, and the difference opens a split along the grain. Checks taper inward. They are visible on the surface but do not penetrate the full depth of the section. A shake is different: a deeper split that follows the medullary rays — the internal radial lines that run from the centre of the log to the bark. In a large post or tie beam, shakes can run some way into the timber and are more visible than checks.

Both look more dramatic than they are. The grading system for structural hardwood framing limits the angle at which the grain runs along any individual piece of timber. Because cracks follow the grain, and the grain angle is controlled by the grade specification, the splits that open up as the fibres dry and separate run along the beam rather than across it. They do not cut through the load-bearing axis of the section. The timber that matters — the wood carrying the structural load along its length — is not compromised.

The structural engineering calculations for a green oak frame already account for this. The strength values used in the calculations are based on the timber at its final, fully seasoned state, after it has done its cracking and movement. The calculations assume the frame will have checked. A frame that has fully cracked and settled is, by the numbers, the frame the engineer calculated for.

According to TRADA's Principles of Green Oak Construction, even large shakes are rarely a structural problem in a properly specified and engineered frame. They may not be desirable on a visible external face from an aesthetic point of view, and that is a reasonable concern. But it is an appearance question, not a structural one.

Oak also gets harder as it dries, not softer. The cracking process and the strengthening process are the same process. The frame you see with cracks running along its faces is a frame that is in the process of becoming denser and more stable.

What to look for

A check tapers inward and does not cross the beam's section. A shake follows an internal ray and may be deeper, but still runs along the grain. Both are normal. A crack that goes cleanly through the full width of a structural member, or appears with visible distortion to the frame around it, is worth a call to your builder. These are uncommon. When in doubt, describe what you are seeing and ask.

The draw-peg mechanism: why movement makes the joints stronger

This is the part that most people do not know, and it is the part that matters most.

The primary joint in an oak frame is the mortice-and-tenon. A tenon is a projecting tongue cut from the end of one timber. A mortice is the corresponding slot cut into a second timber to receive it. The tenon fits into the mortice. A peg, driven through both timbers, locks the joint. This method has been used in British oak framing for many centuries and the reason it has lasted is that it works exceptionally well.

The specific technique used in a quality oak frame is called draw-boring. The peg hole in the mortice and the peg hole in the tenon are drilled at a slight offset from each other, so they do not line up perfectly when the joint is assembled. When the tapered oak peg is then driven through both holes, the offset pulls the tenon deeper into the mortice. The joint tightens mechanically as the peg is driven, in a way that a straight-through peg hole cannot achieve.

The Timber Frame Engineering Council's design guide on draw-boring of pegged joints describes the mechanism directly: "By driving a tapered peg through the off-set holes, the joint can be brought tight together, and kept tight both during the raising and subsequent drying and shrinkage of the members." Evidence of this technique in British oak framing dates from at least the 13th century. It is not a modern refinement. It is the standard craft method.

Now consider the oak in its green state. As the frame dries, the timber shrinks across its grain. The mortice shrinks around the tenon. The timber in which the peg hole sits contracts around the peg. The joint, already drawn tight at raising day, tightens further as the drying process continues. The peg is also cut from fresh oak. It shrinks very little along its length, which means it remains a tightly fitting pin even as the surrounding timber moves around it.

The result is a joint that reaches its maximum tightness not on the day the frame is raised, but in the months and years after. A frame built from seasoned oak is at its tightest the day it is assembled. A green oak frame, jointed and pegged by the draw-boring method, continues to tighten as it dries. The movement you see in the face of the timber is the same process that is pulling the joints together inside it.

The frame is cut from TH1/D30 grade green oak and jointed using mortice-and-tenon, large wedge dovetail, wedge stop splayed, and wedge compound stop splayed scarf joints. The pegs are handmade and tapered. You can read more about the specifics on our oak frame joinery specification page. The point is not to list the joint types for their own sake. The point is that each of them is designed around what the timber does as it dries, not around holding timber in a fixed position while it tries to move.

What to expect in the first three years

Most of the visible movement in a green oak frame happens in the first 18 to 24 months after it is raised. This is the period when the timber is losing most of its moisture and the joints are doing their most active tightening. It is also the period that can be most disconcerting if you are not expecting it.

Warmer, drier conditions accelerate the drying process. A frame in its first summer will show more checking than in a damp autumn. A south-facing post dries faster than a north-facing one, because it receives more direct sun and heat. If the building is kept warm internally, internal timbers dry faster. All of this is normal variation. Different parts of the same frame will dry at different rates, and different faces of the same post will develop different numbers of checks. The frame is responding to its environment.

You may also notice tannin staining. As moisture leaves the wood, it carries natural tannins with it. These can leave dark streaks on masonry, brick, or other materials that sit below or adjacent to the oak. It is a cosmetic effect and it diminishes over time as the tannin content reduces. If you are planning adjacent stonework or paving, mention it to your builder at the design stage so the positioning and fall of any staining can be considered.

After the second year, movement slows considerably. The frame reaches a state of relative equilibrium with its environment. New checks become less frequent. The pegs have done the work they were made for. The frame that looked alarming in its first summer looks settled and characterful in its third or fourth year.

What is normal: surface checks, visible shakes, oak frame shaking or creaking as the joints settle (particularly in the first warm summer), small gaps at infill junctions where the oak has contracted. What is worth a call to your builder: a crack that appears to run cleanly through the full width of a structural post, visible distortion to any part of the frame, or any sign of damp penetration at a weathering junction.

Green oak over time: what the first years make possible

Once the frame has done its drying, the timber is harder and more stable than it was on the day it was raised. The cell structure that held all that moisture has changed. The wood has become denser. It is more resistant to the forces that would work on it over the building's life.

Oak has a natural tannin content of around 8 to 10 percent. This gives it inherent resistance to rot, fungi, and insect attack without any chemical treatment. It is why oak frames can be left exposed externally, weathering to silver over time, without the intervention that would be needed from other timbers. The tannin is not applied. It is part of the wood's chemistry, and it does not diminish in the way a surface treatment would.

The evidence for how a green oak frame ages in situ is visible across the British landscape. Medieval halls, tithe barns, and manor houses raised in the 13th, 14th, and 15th centuries are still in structural use. They cracked when they were raised. The joints tightened as they dried. The timber hardened over decades. The buildings standing today are the result of exactly that process. The movement in the first few years of a green oak frame is not a period of uncertainty before something good happens. It is the beginning of the same process.

A seasoned oak frame jointed at installation is at its tightest the day it is assembled. From that point, any further movement can only loosen it. A green oak frame reaches its maximum joint tightness after drying. The frame the builder raises on day one is not the finished frame. It is the beginning of one. Frequently Asked Questions

Will the cracks in my oak frame get worse over time?

No, and most movement is concentrated in the first two years. As the timber reaches moisture equilibrium with its environment, new checking becomes less frequent and existing cracks stabilise. A frame in a temperate UK setting typically settles considerably by its third or fourth year. Exposed external faces dry faster than internal timbers and may show more movement, but the rate and extent of change slow significantly after the first two summers.

Do the cracks affect the structural strength of the frame?

No. The structural engineering calculations for a green oak frame use strength values based on the timber in its fully seasoned, dried state. The calculations already assume the frame will have cracked before the load values they use are applied. The cracks open along the grain, not across it, so they do not cut through the load-bearing axis of the section. The frame is designed and calculated for what it will become after drying, not for how it looks on the day it is raised.

What is the difference between a check and a shake?

A check is a surface crack that forms as the outer face of a beam dries faster than the core, opening a split along the grain. It tapers inward and does not penetrate the full depth of the section. A shake is a deeper split that follows the medullary rays (the internal radial lines running from the centre of the log to the bark) and can be visible on the face of a large post or tie beam. Both are normal features of a seasoning green oak frame. Neither compromises structural integrity in a properly graded and engineered building.

My frame is making creaking and ticking sounds. Is something moving?

Yes, and this is expected. As the timber dries and the pegged joints settle and tighten, green oak frames make occasional creaking or ticking sounds, particularly in the first summer when warm weather accelerates drying. These are the joints doing what they were designed to do. The sounds diminish as the frame reaches equilibrium. A sudden loud structural crack accompanied by visible movement of the frame would be different. If you are at all uncertain, contact your builder and describe what you heard and where.

Will cracks in the timber let water in?

Checks and shakes on internal timbers present no weather risk. On external structural members, larger shakes that are open to driving rain on their upper faces can, in some circumstances, allow water entry. A well-designed frame accounts for this through the orientation of the members and the weathering detailing around them. This is one reason why the positioning and orientation of external structural timbers should be discussed with your builder at the design stage, not treated as something to address after the frame is raised.

How long does it take for a green oak frame to fully season?

Most significant movement happens in the first 18 to 24 months after the frame is raised. Movement slows considerably after that as the timber reaches equilibrium with its environment. Full seasoning — where the timber has stabilised at a moisture content consistent with its setting — takes longer, typically several years for large structural section sizes. A well-specified green oak frame continues to harden for many years after the most visible movement has settled.
