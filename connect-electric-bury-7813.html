ECO4 is no longer available: what this means for Greater Manchester buyers

The Energy Company Obligation scheme, fourth phase (ECO4), ran from April 2022 to 31 March 2026. It placed legal obligations on large energy suppliers to fund energy efficiency improvements, including solar PV panels, for qualifying low-income households. As confirmed on Manchester City Council's energy efficiency grants page, ECO4 ran until 31 March 2026. That date has passed. The scheme is closed to new applications.

Solar was always a relatively small part of ECO4. The scheme's primary focus was insulation and heating upgrades. Solar PV was only eligible under specific conditions. The property needed a qualifying heating system in place or installed as part of the project. Most of the ECO4 solar installations that did happen were attached to heat pump or storage heater upgrades, not standalone solar grants for average-income households.

If you search for solar panel grants today, you will find dozens of websites presenting ECO4 as still open, with eligibility checkers and urgency messages. They have not been updated. We turned away enquiries from buyers in early 2026 who had spent weeks trying to apply to a scheme that was already closed. Do not submit personal details to any service claiming ECO4 solar is currently available in Greater Manchester.

The Warm Homes Local Grant: what it covers and who qualifies in Greater Manchester

The Warm Homes Local Grant is the government's replacement for ECO4. Unlike ECO4, which was funded and delivered by energy suppliers, the Warm Homes Local Grant is a government capital programme delivered through local authorities. The DESNZ guidance on the Warm Homes Local Grant confirms £500 million has been allocated nationally, with Greater Manchester Combined Authority (GMCA) receiving a specific allocation under the government's Warm Homes and Public Sector Decarbonisation Devolution Programme.

In Greater Manchester, the scheme is primarily targeted at households in postcodes falling within Income Deciles 1 or 2 of the Indices of Multiple Deprivation 2025, which are the most economically deprived areas. Households outside these postcodes can still qualify if they receive means-tested benefits (Universal Credit, Pension Credit, Housing Benefit, income-based JSA or ESA, Child Tax Credit, or Working Tax Credit) or if their gross household income is below £36,000 per year. The property must also have an EPC rating of D, E, F, or G.

Measures covered include solar PV, battery storage, heat pumps, and insulation, up to £15,000 per household. The funding covers the full cost of qualifying measures, with no contribution required from the homeowner. Measures are recommended by a Retrofit Assessment, not chosen by the homeowner. Private landlords registered with the Good Landlord Charter can also apply, with grants of up to £30,000 for private rented properties. The scheme is administered via the GMCA portal and local council energy teams.

What if you don't qualify for the Warm Homes Local Grant?

Most Greater Manchester homeowners who search "solar panel grants" will not qualify. The income threshold of £36,000 and the EPC and postcode requirements are deliberately targeted at households in fuel poverty, not at average-income buyers who have looked into solar as a financial decision. That is worth stating plainly. If your household income is above £36,000 or your EPC is rated C or above, the Warm Homes Local Grant will not be available to you. Buyers who spend weeks chasing a grant route they don't qualify for delay a solar decision that would otherwise have a strong financial case on its own.

There are two financial incentives available to every homeowner regardless of income. The first is 0% VAT on residential solar panel installation. As confirmed in HMRC's VAT Notice 708/6 on energy-saving materials, the zero rate applies to residential solar PV installation from 1 May 2023 to 31 March 2027. After that date, the rate reverts to 5%. No application is required. Any VAT-registered, MCS-certified installer applies the rate automatically. On a typical 4 kW system installed in Greater Manchester, the VAT saving is approximately £1,000 to £1,200.

The second is the Smart Export Guarantee (SEG). Once your panels are installed, the SEG pays you for surplus electricity exported to the grid. This requires your system to be MCS-certified and for you to have a smart meter. You apply to a licensed SEG supplier, which can be different from your electricity import supplier. Rates vary, so compare before registering. The Energy Saving Trust's guide to the Smart Export Guarantee covers the application process and where to compare current rates.

What MCS certification means for your grant and SEG eligibility

For both the Warm Homes Local Grant and the Smart Export Guarantee, MCS certification is not optional. Under the Ofgem Smart Export Guarantee guidance for generators, installations must be MCS-certified to register for export payments. Without MCS certification, the system cannot be registered with a SEG supplier and the owner receives no export income. For the Warm Homes Local Grant, all funded installations must also be MCS-certified. It is a requirement of the scheme, not an optional quality mark.

MCS (Microgeneration Certification Scheme) certifies the quality and standards compliance of small-scale energy installations. Installers must hold a valid MCS certificate and demonstrate ongoing competence through the scheme. Connect Electric is MCS-certified under NAP 14690. This number is verifiable on the MCS installer directory at mcscertified.com. When comparing quotes, check the installer's MCS number before signing anything. A non-MCS-certified installation locks you out of the SEG for the life of the system, and that is a significant financial consequence over a 25-year panel lifespan.

We see this in practice more than buyers realise. A system installed without MCS certification, sometimes by builders or general electricians who take on solar work, can still generate electricity, but the owner cannot access SEG payments and would not qualify for Warm Homes Local Grant funding. Getting the certification later is complex and sometimes not possible. It is one of the most important things to check before installation begins.

The self-funded case for solar in Greater Manchester

Greater Manchester receives approximately 850 to 950 kWh of solar irradiance per kilowatt-peak per year, based on PVGIS data for North West England. That is less than Cornwall, but comparable to much of the Midlands and enough for solar to deliver a strong return across the typical 25-year panel lifespan. A 4 kW south-facing system in Greater Manchester typically generates around 3,100 to 3,300 kWh per year, enough to cover a significant share of household electricity consumption if usage patterns align with generation.

At a typical grid import rate of around 24p per kWh (Ofgem Q2 2026 price cap figures), each kWh of self-consumed solar electricity replaces grid import at that rate. SEG export adds income on top for surplus generation. With 0% VAT in place until 31 March 2027, 2026 is the year to lock in the cost before the rate changes. The financial case does not depend on a grant. It depends on your roof, your tariff, and how your household uses electricity through the day.

Does 0% VAT on solar panels apply to everyone?

Yes. The 0% VAT rate on residential solar panel installation is available to all homeowners regardless of income. It is confirmed in HMRC's VAT Notice 708/6 and applies until 31 March 2027. After that date, VAT reverts to 5%. No application is needed. Any VAT-registered MCS-certified installer applies the rate automatically at invoice. On a typical 4 kW system in Greater Manchester, the saving is approximately £1,000 to £1,200.

What is the Smart Export Guarantee and how do I get it?

The Smart Export Guarantee (SEG) pays you for surplus electricity your solar panels export to the grid. It is administered by Ofgem and requires your system to be MCS-certified and you to have a smart meter. You apply directly to a licensed SEG supplier, which can be a different supplier from the one that provides your import electricity. Rates vary between suppliers, so compare before registering. Ofgem publishes the current list of SEG licensees annually. The Energy Saving Trust also publishes guidance on comparing rates.
