Why MCS Certification Is the Non-Negotiable Starting Point

MCS, the Microgeneration Certification Scheme, is the UK's quality mark for small-scale renewable energy installations including heat pumps, solar PV panels and battery storage. An MCS-certified installer has demonstrated compliance with recognised installation standards, carries appropriate insurance, and is assessed on a regular cycle. For a homeowner, this matters because without it, the main financial benefits of both technologies are closed off to you.

For heat pumps, MCS certification unlocks the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant. The grant is up to £7,500 for air-to-water systems, and the application is handled through your installer. Your MCS-certified installer applies to Ofgem and deducts the grant from your invoice at the point of sale. The Energy Saving Trust's guide to the Boiler Upgrade Scheme confirms that installations must be carried out by an MCS-certified installer as a condition of eligibility.

For solar panels, MCS certification is what qualifies your installation for the Smart Export Guarantee. The SEG pays you for surplus electricity your panels send to the grid, and every licensed energy supplier with over 150,000 customers is required to offer an SEG tariff. As the Energy Saving Trust's SEG guide states, the technology and installer must be certified under MCS or equivalent. Without that certification, most energy suppliers will reject your SEG application, and you are unlikely to qualify for any regulated export payments however well your panels perform.

You can verify any installer's current MCS status using the public MCS installer search at mcscertified.com. Search by postcode or company name, then filter by the specific technology being quoted. When we work with homeowners across Tyne and Wear, we show our MCS certificate at the survey. You should expect any north east solar or heat pump installer you speak to to do the same.

MCS certification is technology-specific

An installer can hold MCS for solar PV but not for heat pumps, or for battery storage but not for ground source systems. Before agreeing to any survey, confirm that the firm holds current certification for exactly the technology they are proposing to install. The MCS installer search tool lets you filter by technology type so you can check this in under a minute.

A good survey is where a credible installation begins. But before you agree to a survey with any north east heat pump installer or solar company, these five questions tell you a lot about how they work.

First: will you give me a fixed written price before any work starts? A verbal quote, or one that arrives with "subject to survey findings" attached, is not a fixed price and can grow. For our air source heat pump installation in the North East, we give you a fixed written proposal before you agree to anything. If the price changes at any stage, that is on us.

Second: for heat pumps, will you carry out a room-by-room heat-loss calculation before sizing the system? For solar, will you assess roof orientation, pitch and shading before quoting? Neither question should require hesitation. Without the heat-loss calculation, a heat pump size is a guess. Without the roof and shading assessment, a solar panel count may be wrong for your specific property.

Third: are you MCS-certified for the specific technology you are quoting me? Confirm it covers the exact system, not just a related one.

Fourth: are you a member of RECC or another TSI-approved consumer code? This matters for your protection if anything goes wrong after installation.

Fifth: for solar, will you advise me on registering for the Smart Export Guarantee and help me compare SEG tariffs? Getting you onto an export tariff is part of the job, not an afterthought.

Sizing for Your Home: Solar Surveys and Heat-Loss Calculations

The single biggest source of underperforming installations is a system sized for a generic property rather than the actual one. For both solar and heat pumps, the survey is where the design either earns its place or lets you down.

For solar panels, sizing starts with the roof. Orientation matters: a south-facing roof at a pitch of around 30 to 40 degrees generates the most energy, but east-west split arrays work well for homes that use more electricity in mornings and evenings. Shading is just as important. A chimney, a tree or a neighbouring roofline that shades even part of the panels for part of the day will reduce output, and a competent installer models this before quoting. We have surveyed homes across Tyne and Wear where a national quote assumed a straightforward south-facing roof and significantly miscounted the panels for how that property actually sits. The survey is where north east solar installers either design something that performs, or cut corners that cost you every year.

For heat pumps, sizing starts with a heat-loss calculation. This is a room-by-room assessment of how much heat the property loses, based on wall construction, window area, insulation, floor type and ceiling height. It tells the installer what output the heat pump needs to keep the home warm on the coldest day of the year. Get it wrong and you pay twice: an oversized pump costs more to buy and runs inefficiently, an undersized one struggles when you need it most. We have seen homes across the North East where the buyer found a heat pump installer nearby, the system went in without a proper heat-loss calculation, and it ran at flow temperatures too high for the radiators, costing more to run than the old gas boiler.

The sizing trap: what goes wrong when the survey is skipped

An oversized battery sits half-empty for years while you pay off the finance. An undersized heat pump struggles on the coldest days. A solar system designed without accounting for shading underperforms year-round. All three come from quotes that skipped the survey. Ask any installer to walk you through how they arrived at the system size before you sign anything.

Red Flags That Mean Walk Away

We would rather you read this section and not need it. Most north east solar and heat pump companies are doing the job properly. But the ones that are not tend to follow a pattern, and knowing the warning signs before you have agreed to anything is worth your time.

A quote with no home visit

For solar, shading and roof pitch cannot be accurately assessed from a satellite image. For heat pumps, a heat-loss calculation cannot be done remotely. No site visit means no real design, whatever the quote looks like.

No written price

If a quote is verbal, or qualified by "subject to survey," ask for it in writing with the final number locked. If the installer cannot do that, move on.

Grant urgency

"The scheme closes soon," "this price is only valid if you sign today," "we only have one slot left." These are pressure tactics. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme runs to 2030. A genuine installer does not need to rush you.

No shading analysis for solar

If an installer quotes for panels without discussing how shading affects your specific roof across the year, they are not designing for your property. Ask them directly: how did you account for shading on my roof?

No questions about existing kit

Any installer quoting for a heat pump should ask about solar panels, batteries or solar thermal already in the home before designing anything. We had a customer with solar PV, battery storage and solar thermal already fitted when they came to us. We designed the heat pump around what they had. An installer who does not ask is not doing the full job.

A competitive quote backed by a site visit, a proper design, and a fixed written price is worth taking seriously. A cheap verbal estimate from someone who has never visited your home is not, however low it comes in.

Any installer you speak to should ask about your tenure before quoting for either technology, not after.

What RECC Membership and Consumer Code Protection Actually Mean

RECC, the Renewable Energy Consumer Code, is a consumer protection scheme approved by the Chartered Trading Standards Institute. It sets enforceable standards for how a renewable energy firm markets its services, provides pre-contract information, quotes, handles deposits, and what happens if something goes wrong after installation.

Under the Renewable Energy Consumer Code, member firms must give written quotations before any commitment, offer deposit and workmanship warranty protection, and provide access to independent dispute resolution if a complaint cannot be resolved directly. That last point is the one most homeowners overlook. If something goes wrong with a RECC member installer and you cannot resolve it, you have a route to independent resolution without going to a solicitor or a court. That route does not exist with an unaffiliated firm.

MCS certification requires installers to hold membership of a Trading Standards Institute-approved consumer code, of which RECC is the main one for renewables. A genuinely MCS-certified installer should be able to name their consumer code without hesitation. If they cannot, check before you proceed. We are RECC members, and we think that protection should be standard, not a differentiator.

Do I need MCS certification to register for the Smart Export Guarantee with solar panels?

Yes, in practice. The Smart Export Guarantee scheme requires your installation to be MCS-certified or to meet an equivalent standard. Without it, most energy suppliers will reject your SEG application and you will not receive regulated export payments for electricity your panels send to the grid. Your installer provides an MCS certificate on completion, which you use when registering for an SEG tariff. Rates vary by supplier and change frequently, so compare options at the time you register rather than accepting the first tariff offered.

Can I claim the £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant myself?

No. The BUS grant application is handled through your MCS-certified installer, who applies to Ofgem and deducts the grant from your invoice. You cannot apply directly. If the installer you hire is not MCS-certified, the grant is not available to you regardless of the quality of the work. Before agreeing to any heat pump installation, confirm the installer holds current MCS certification for air-to-water heat pumps and will handle the BUS application as part of the job. What is RECC and why does it matter when choosing a solar or heat pump installer?

RECC stands for the Renewable Energy Consumer Code. It is a consumer protection scheme approved by the Chartered Trading Standards Institute, covering pre-contract information, written quotes, deposit protection, workmanship warranties, and dispute resolution. The practical value is that if something goes wrong with a RECC member installer and you cannot resolve it directly, you can access independent dispute resolution without going to court. MCS certification requires installers to hold membership of a TSI-approved consumer code, of which RECC is the main one for renewables.

I already have solar panels. Does that affect what heat pump I can have?

It should affect how the heat pump is designed, not whether you can have one. A heat pump draws electricity to run, and existing solar generation can reduce what you import from the grid during the day. For this to work well, the heat pump needs to be designed around your existing solar and battery setup, not quoted without reference to it. Tell any installer about your solar array, battery if you have one, and any solar thermal panels before they begin designing. An installer who does not ask is not doing the full job.
