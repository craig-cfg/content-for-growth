My Solar Inverter Has a Fault Code. What Does It Mean and What Should I Do?

Solar inverter fault codes fall into three categories. Isolation or insulation faults (labelled Riso Low, Earth Fault, or similar) indicate a failure in the DC wiring's insulation and require a qualified engineer today, not next week. Grid faults, over-temperature faults, and AC voltage warnings are usually temporary, self-clearing conditions, but are worth monitoring, and recurring patterns should be investigated. Communication faults, labelled as no monitoring or gateway error, almost always mean the monitoring system has lost its internet connection, not that the solar inverter has failed.

Your solar inverter is showing a fault code and you are not sure what it means. Before you call anyone, it helps to know that fault codes are not all the same kind of problem. Some mean the system has paused temporarily and will restart without any action. Some mean there is a pattern developing that is worth investigating in the next few days. And some mean you should not restart the system and should call a qualified engineer today.

The guide below covers the main fault categories in the order we check them when a homeowner calls. It is not a lookup table for specific brand codes, because inverter manufacturers use different numbering systems and the code on your display may not match what another brand calls the same condition. What matters first is the category.

First: what does the fault light actually mean?

Most solar inverters use a three-colour status light system. Green means the system is operating normally. Amber or orange means a warning has been detected but the system may still be generating. Red means the inverter has shut down and stopped generating. No light at all usually means the inverter has no power, which points to a problem at the consumer unit or the DC disconnect rather than the inverter itself.

The colour tells you roughly how serious the situation is. The code on the display tells you what the situation actually is. These are two different pieces of information, and you need both.

Before you do anything else, take a photograph of the inverter display showing the fault code. The code disappears when the system restarts or resets, and the engineer who diagnoses it, whether remotely or on site, will need to know the exact code. A photograph of the screen taken before any reset saves half the diagnostic process. When we get a call about a fault, the first thing we ask is what the display shows.

Once you have the code photographed, look up the category in your inverter's manual or the manufacturer's app. Most apps give a plain-language description alongside the code. If the description puts it in one of the categories below, you will know what to do next.

Grid faults: the most common codes, and when they matter

Grid fault codes are the most frequently reported inverter fault in the UK. They appear when the inverter detects that the voltage or frequency of the electricity coming from the grid has moved outside the range it is required to operate within.

This is not an inverter failure. It is the inverter doing exactly what it is supposed to do. Under the G98 engineering standard that governs grid-connected solar installations, every residential solar inverter must disconnect from the network if voltage or frequency moves outside the permitted range. The reason is safety: an inverter that continues to feed electricity into the grid during a network fault could endanger the engineers working to fix it. G98 is set by the Energy Networks Association and administered locally by the Distribution Network Operator.

A grid fault code that appears once and then clears is almost certainly a temporary network event. Your inverter disconnected, the grid returned to normal, and the system restarted automatically. No action is needed.

Recurring grid faults on sunny afternoons: what is usually happening

If your grid fault codes appear repeatedly in the afternoon on bright sunny days, the cause is often local network voltage rising too high as multiple solar systems in the area export electricity to the grid simultaneously. The inverter is protecting itself and complying with G98 by disconnecting. This is not a fault with your system. However, if it happens frequently enough to reduce your generation meaningfully, it is worth mentioning to your installer or a solar engineer. In some cases inverter export settings can be adjusted, but this requires reading the data first rather than guessing at settings changes.

A grid fault code that appears every day, or at the same time of day, or in response to specific conditions, is a pattern worth investigating. It may indicate the local grid voltage is consistently elevated, which is worth reporting to your DNO. We see this regularly in areas with high solar density, and we can read the fault history from the inverter to confirm whether it is a recurring condition before recommending anything.

Isolation faults (Riso Low): do not wait on these

An isolation fault, labelled Riso Low, Insulation Fault, Earth Fault, or similar depending on the brand, means the inverter has detected that the electrical resistance between the DC circuit and earth has dropped below a safe minimum.

The DC circuit is the high-voltage direct current side of your solar system: the wiring from the panels down to the inverter. Solar panels in the UK typically produce between 300 and 600 volts DC depending on system size and configuration. That voltage is present whenever there is daylight, even if the AC output has been switched off at the consumer unit. It cannot be isolated by switching the inverter off.

An isolation fault means something in that DC circuit has failed. The most common causes are water ingress at a connector, damage to the cable sheathing where it runs across the roof or through the roof penetration, or a failed component inside a junction box. Any of these allows current to find an unintended path to earth, which is an electrical safety condition.

This category of fault requires a qualified engineer to identify and fix the source before the system is restarted. Do not reset the inverter and do not attempt to inspect DC wiring yourself. The voltage on the DC side of the system is present and dangerous regardless of what the inverter display shows.

When someone calls us and the answer to our first question is yes, it is an insulation or isolation fault, the conversation about timing changes immediately. These do not wait until next week. We carry out solar panel repairs on systems of any age and any original installer, and we can usually get to an isolation fault within a few days of the call.

If your inverter is showing an insulation or isolation fault, do not restart the system. Contact a qualified solar engineer and arrange a visit before the inverter is touched.

Over-temperature faults: usually self-clearing, but worth understanding

Solar inverters generate heat and are designed to reduce output or shut down temporarily if the internal temperature reaches a threshold. An over-temperature fault that appears on a hot summer afternoon and clears by early evening is normal inverter self-protection. The system will restart when it cools down and resume generating. No action is needed other than noting that it happened.

What is worth paying attention to is a recurring over-temperature pattern, particularly if it appears in mild weather, in the morning rather than the afternoon, or in a space that should be well ventilated. Recurring over-temperature faults in conditions that should not stress the inverter usually indicate one of three things: the inverter is in a poorly ventilated location where heat is building up, something has changed in the environment since installation, or a component inside the inverter is starting to fail and generating excess heat.

The practical checks worth doing before calling an engineer: confirm there is clear space around the inverter on all sides, typically 20cm or more, and nothing has been stored in front of it or on top of it. Check whether the space has become warmer since installation. We have had calls where a boiler was installed next to an inverter after the solar was commissioned, or boxes had been gradually moved to fill the space around it. Moving the obstruction solved a recurring fault that had been happening for months. Worth checking before booking a visit.

If the space is clear and the fault is still recurring in mild conditions, book a diagnostic visit. Inverters that are overheating internally without environmental cause are usually indicating a component problem, and catching it early gives you more options than waiting for a complete failure.

Communication faults: the inverter is probably fine

A communication fault, labelled as no monitoring, connection lost, gateway offline, or similar, means the monitoring system has lost contact with the internet. This is different from the inverter losing the ability to generate electricity. In most cases the inverter is working normally and generation is continuing. The app is simply not receiving data.

The monitoring component is either a separate gateway box, usually located near the router, or an integrated WiFi module inside the inverter. Both can lose their connection for the same reasons any internet-connected device loses its connection: the broadband has dropped, the router has been restarted and the device has not reconnected, or the WiFi password has changed.

The restart sequence worth trying before calling anyone: check that your broadband router is working normally. Restart the monitoring gateway by switching it off at the mains and waiting thirty seconds before switching it back on. Wait ten minutes. In most cases the system reconnects and the app resumes showing data. About half the calls we receive that start with "my inverter is not working" turn out to be monitoring faults, not inverter faults. The system is generating, the app is just not receiving the data.

If the system has not been reporting for more than a few days and the restart does not resolve it, the monitoring gateway may need reconfiguring or replacing. This is a relatively straightforward fix and does not necessarily mean the inverter itself needs attention. If you are unsure, book a diagnostic visit and we can check both the inverter and the monitoring system at the same time as part of our solar panel maintenance service.

AC faults, output faults, and persistent codes that will not clear

AC faults, high AC voltage, low AC voltage, frequency deviation, are similar in origin to grid faults but reflect a condition on the output side of the inverter. Most resolve when the grid returns to normal and do not need action beyond the usual monitoring approach. If they recur persistently, the connection between the inverter and the consumer unit may have developed a fault, or the inverter's AC output stage may have a component issue.

It is also worth noting the financial consequence of a fault that goes unresolved. While the inverter is not generating, you are not earning Smart Export Guarantee payments for any electricity that would otherwise have been exported to the grid. On a system that has been offline for several weeks without the homeowner realising, the missed export income adds up. Checking the monitoring app regularly, or setting up fault alerts where the system supports it, catches problems before they become expensive silently.

Persistent fault codes that do not clear after a restart, and do not fall into the grid fault or communication fault categories, are the signal to stop self-diagnosing and book a visit. The temptation is to keep resetting the inverter to see if the code clears, but repeated resets without fixing the underlying cause can mask what the inverter is trying to tell you and, in some cases, worsen a developing component fault.

When we plug into an inverter for diagnostics, we can read the fault history stored in the inverter's memory. Most inverters hold several hundred timestamped fault events. That history tells us whether a fault is a one-off, a pattern, or a fault that has been repeatedly cleared and reoccurred. It tells us whether the issue is a firmware update that the installer should have applied, a component approaching failure, or a wiring fault that needs fixing at the roof. We cannot get that information from a description of what the display says. We can get it in most cases from a diagnostic visit.

Frequently Asked Questions

My inverter light has turned red. Does that mean I need a new inverter?

Not necessarily. A red light means the inverter has detected a fault and stopped generating, but the cause could be a temporary grid condition, a communication issue, or a component fault that is repairable rather than terminal. Before assuming the inverter needs replacing, it is worth identifying the fault code on the display and checking which category it falls into. Many red-light faults clear on their own, and many that do not can be repaired at considerably less cost than a full replacement. A diagnostic visit reads the inverter's fault history and gives a clear answer on which applies in your case.

My solar app says my system is not generating but the inverter light is green. What is happening?

This is almost always a communication fault between the monitoring gateway and the internet, not a problem with the inverter itself. The inverter continues to generate electricity, but the monitoring system is not receiving or transmitting data, so the app shows zero or no data. Try restarting the monitoring gateway by switching it off and back on. Wait ten minutes and check whether the app begins showing data again. If the inverter light is green and the system is running normally in every other respect, the generation is almost certainly continuing, the monitoring is not.

How do I know if my solar inverter fault code is serious?

The fault category matters more than the specific code number. Isolation or insulation faults (Riso Low, Earth Fault, or similar) are serious and require a qualified engineer before the system is restarted. Grid faults, AC voltage warnings, and over-temperature faults that clear on their own are usually temporary and do not require immediate action, but recurring patterns should be investigated. Communication faults are usually monitoring system problems, not inverter failures. Photograph the fault code on the display, note whether it clears on its own or persists after a restart, and whether it appears at the same time of day or under specific conditions.

Can I reset my solar inverter myself to clear a fault code?

For most fault types, yes. Restarting the inverter by switching it off at the isolator switch, waiting thirty seconds, and switching it back on is a reasonable first step for grid faults, over-temperature faults, and communication faults. If the fault clears and does not return, no further action is needed. If the fault reappears immediately after a reset, or reappears regularly, a diagnostic visit is the next step. The exception is isolation and insulation faults. Do not attempt to reset the inverter if it is showing an isolation or earth fault. The DC side of the system carries dangerous voltage and requires a qualified engineer to inspect and fix the underlying cause before the system is restarted.

What does Riso Low mean on a solar inverter?

Riso Low is an isolation resistance fault. It means the insulation resistance between the DC circuit (your solar panels and their wiring) and earth has dropped below the minimum safe level the inverter requires. The DC side of a solar system carries significant voltage even when the AC output has been disconnected, so this is an electrical safety condition rather than a routine maintenance issue. The most common causes are water ingress at a connector, damaged cable insulation, or a failed component in a panel junction box. A qualified solar engineer needs to identify and fix the source of the fault before the inverter is restarted.

How long does a solar inverter diagnostic check take?

Most inverter diagnostic visits take between one and two hours on site. The first step is connecting to the inverter and reading its fault history, which shows every fault event with a timestamp over the past several months. This takes around twenty minutes and usually gives a clear picture of whether the issue is a one-off event, a recurring pattern, or a developing component failure. If the diagnosis points to a repair that can be carried out on the same visit, most straightforward inverter faults can be resolved on the day. If the inverter needs replacing, we can confirm this during the diagnostic visit and usually complete the replacement without requiring a second appointment.
