"I Thought I Was Just Bad at Life" — and Then I Was Assessed for ADHD

A thorough adult ADHD assessment examines both your current difficulties and your childhood history, because ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition that must have been present before the age of 12. A structured clinical interview, validated diagnostic tools such as the DIVA-5, and an account from someone who knew you in childhood are all part of a rigorous evaluation. The outcome is a diagnostic report with a clinical formulation and treatment recommendations, not simply a yes or no.

If you have spent most of your adult life being told, or telling yourself, that you are disorganised, inconsistent, or simply not trying hard enough, you are not unusual. For a significant number of adults, an ADHD assessment in their thirties or forties reveals something that puts years of struggle into a very different frame. The label does not change what happened. But it does change how you understand it, and that matters more than people expect.

This article is for adults who suspect ADHD but have not yet acted on that suspicion, and for those who have already had an experience of assessment and found it underwhelming. It explains what a genuine ADHD assessment involves, why the assessment matters as much as the diagnosis, and what to look for in a provider who will take the time the condition requires.

Why so many adults are only now finding out they have ADHD

The scale of unmet need is not a matter of debate. NHS England's ADHD waiting list data for November 2025 recorded up to 700,123 people who may be waiting for an assessment in England as of September 2025, a rise of more than 20% on the same month the previous year. In some parts of the country, waiting times have grown to ten years or more. The demand is not manufactured. It reflects something that has been invisible for decades and is now, slowly, becoming visible.

Part of what made it invisible was gender. The NHS England ADHD Taskforce report on under-diagnosis and waiting times (2025) is direct: women and girls are much less likely to have an ADHD diagnosis. The historical research on ADHD was conducted almost entirely on boys, who presented with the hyperactive and disruptive behaviours that made the condition unmissable in a classroom. Girls and women with ADHD often present differently. Inattention rather than hyperactivity. Emotional dysregulation that looks, to everyone including their own doctors, like anxiety. A tendency to internalise rather than act out. And, crucially, an ability to mask that is so well developed by the time they reach adulthood that they have convinced everyone around them, including often themselves, that they are simply not very good at managing life. A large proportion of the adults who come to us for assessment have already had years of anxiety or depression treatment before anyone raised ADHD as a possibility. That pattern, in our experience, is far more common in women than in men.

The result is a generation of adults, many of them women, who spent childhood being called bright but inconsistent, forgetful, too sensitive, or difficult to motivate. Who spent their twenties and thirties building elaborate scaffolding around their difficulties without knowing that was what they were doing. And who are arriving at assessment now, often exhausted, relieved to have a name for it, and sometimes quietly furious that it took this long.

What ADHD actually looks like in adults, and why it is so easy to miss

Adult ADHD rarely looks like the hyperactive child in the classroom. In adults, particularly in women and in people who have developed strong compensatory strategies over many years, the presentation is usually subtler. Difficulty starting tasks that feel boring or overwhelming. A tendency to hyperfocus on things that are genuinely interesting, which can look like the opposite of inattention. Losing track of time in ways that feel humiliating rather than innocent. Emotional responses that arrive faster and stronger than the situation seems to warrant. A constant background sense of almost, but never quite, keeping up.

Many adults who come to assessment have already been treated for anxiety or depression. That history matters, and we take it seriously. Both conditions can be real, co-occurring, and in genuine need of treatment. They can also be the downstream effect of spending decades trying to manage a brain that was never given the right explanation for why things that come easily to other people require so much more effort from you. Exhaustion and low self-worth accumulate. Anxiety fills the gap where structure should be. Being told, after years of anxiety treatment, that ADHD was underneath it all along can be disorienting, and we recognise that. A thorough assessment does not dismiss what came before. It tries to find the most accurate explanation for the full picture, and whether more than one thing is true at once.

Many people also arrive having already spent significant time with self-screening tools or reading other people's accounts on social media. Those can be genuinely useful starting points. The gap between recognising yourself in a description and meeting the clinical criteria for ADHD is, however, exactly where a proper assessment lives.

Why the assessment itself matters as much as the diagnosis

ADHD is a clinical diagnosis. NICE guideline NG87 on ADHD diagnosis and management, which sets the UK standard for assessment, is clear that diagnosis requires specialist input, evidence that symptoms were present before the age of 12, and evidence of clinically significant functional impairment across multiple settings, not just one area of life. Screening tools and rating scales can contribute to an assessment. They cannot replace it.

This matters practically. A diagnosis reached in a single online session without a developmental history, without an informant account, and without differential diagnosis is a diagnosis that cannot adequately distinguish ADHD from anxiety, from burnout, from the effects of trauma, or from the combination of all of these. NHS GPs, when asked to support a shared care arrangement for medication, are increasingly attentive to the quality of the underlying assessment. A comprehensive, specialist report is more likely to be accepted. A brief online summary is often not.

There is a more fundamental reason too. An assessment that explores your history, your patterns, your compensatory strategies, and the full picture of your life tells you something far more useful than a binary outcome. It tells you why things work the way they do for you, not just whether they carry a particular label. That understanding is often where the real value of a proper assessment lies, independent of whether the diagnosis itself is ADHD or something else.

We would rather take the time an accurate assessment requires than provide a quick answer that does not hold up. That position is slower. It is also clinically more honest.

What a rigorous private ADHD assessment involves

A thorough assessment has several components, and the depth of each matters as much as the order. Our approach follows the UK Adult ADHD Network (UKAAN) quality assurance standard, which defines what a high-quality assessment should include and why.

Before your first appointment, you complete pre-assessment questionnaires and validated rating scales covering your symptoms and their impact on daily life. We also request information from someone who knew you well in childhood, typically a parent or sibling. That informant account is not a formality. Because ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition with childhood onset, evidence of how you functioned before the age of 12 is clinically important. School reports, where they exist, add further weight.

The structured clinical interview is the core of the assessment. We use the DIVA-5, the Diagnostic Interview for ADHD in Adults, a structured clinical interview that explores ADHD symptoms in childhood and adulthood, including inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, age of onset, persistence and impairment across daily life. Emotional regulation and co-occurring difficulties are explored as part of the wider psychiatric formulation. This takes approximately two hours. The time is not an inconvenience. It is the point. A 60-minute online session is unlikely to achieve what two hours of structured clinical conversation allows.

The psychiatric assessment follows. A Consultant Psychiatrist reviews the evidence, considers co-occurring conditions, explores differential diagnosis, and makes the clinical judgement about whether the picture supports a diagnosis of ADHD. The written report covers conclusions, recommendations, and, where relevant, information about medication, psychological support, and shared care. That report is what your GP receives if shared care is proposed, and what an employer or educational institution may request if formal accommodations are needed.

If you choose our combined pathway, detailed on our adult ADHD service page, a clinical psychologist contributes a formulation alongside the psychiatric assessment: not just a diagnosis, but a coherent account of how your developmental history, your current circumstances, and your specific patterns fit together. Many adults who come to us say the formulation is the part that stays with them. The diagnosis opens a door. The formulation tells you what is on the other side.

After the assessment: what the next steps actually look like

A diagnosis opens conversations rather than closing a chapter. Many adults need time to sit with the formulation before making any decisions about treatment. That is entirely reasonable, and we do not treat the report as a starting gun for prescribing.

Where medication is clinically appropriate, it is prescribed and titrated by a Consultant Psychiatrist. That means a full discussion of the evidence, the different medication classes available, expected effects, and how to manage the titration period. Ongoing prescriptions can be managed in several ways: continued prescribing by our psychiatric team, delegation to the in-house GP service, or a shared care agreement with your NHS GP where that GP is willing and your local NHS area permits it. That last option has become less straightforward as NHS pressures have increased, and it is honest to say that shared care cannot be guaranteed. A comprehensive multidisciplinary report following UKAAN standards gives the best possible basis for that conversation, but it does not guarantee the outcome.

Psychological support alongside or instead of medication is available through our psychology and counselling service. For adults with ADHD, this typically means cognitive behavioural therapy approaches adapted for ADHD, practical strategies for time management and emotional regulation, and support for the emotional work that often follows a late diagnosis. Understanding why the last 20 or 30 years played out the way they did can bring real relief. It can also bring grief. Having someone to work through that with is not an optional add-on. For many people, it is what makes the rest sustainable.

Finding the right assessment: questions worth asking any provider

If you are still deciding where to go, there are a few things worth asking before you book.

Does the assessment follow NICE guideline NG87 and the UKAAN quality standard? Does the process include a structured childhood developmental history? In our experience, this is the question that most quickly distinguishes thorough providers from superficial ones: clinics that conduct ADHD assessments without exploring childhood history are not following the evidence base, and a diagnosis built on current symptoms alone does not meet the clinical standard. Is there a full psychiatric component, or does the assessment rely on questionnaires and a brief review? Does the report include a clinical formulation, or just a binary outcome? Is the report written with shared care in mind?

None of these questions is adversarial. They are the questions a thorough provider will expect and answer without hesitation. A clinic that hesitates or deflects on them is telling you something useful. A clinic that answers them directly is one worth talking to.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does an adult ADHD assessment involve in the UK?

A rigorous adult ADHD assessment in the UK follows NICE guideline NG87 and includes a structured clinical interview, a detailed exploration of childhood developmental history, an account from someone who knew you in childhood where possible, and a full psychiatric assessment that considers differential diagnosis. Validated tools such as the DIVA-5 are used to structure the clinical conversation. The outcome is a written report with a diagnosis where appropriate, a clinical formulation, and treatment recommendations.

How long does a private adult ADHD assessment take?

A combined psychology and psychiatry assessment typically involves a two-hour structured clinical interview with a psychologist, followed by a separate psychiatric assessment of approximately 60 to 90 minutes. This multi-appointment structure is deliberate. Adequate time is needed to explore developmental history, assess co-occurring conditions, and reach a well-grounded diagnostic formulation. An assessment that takes significantly less time is unlikely to achieve what a thorough assessment requires.

Why are women with ADHD often diagnosed later in life?

Historical ADHD research focused predominantly on boys, and the inattentive presentation more common in girls and women was less well recognised. Women are also more likely to develop masking strategies over time, appearing organised and high-functioning while working much harder than their peers to maintain that appearance. Emotional dysregulation, which is often prominent in women with ADHD, is frequently attributed to anxiety or a mood difficulty instead. The NHS England ADHD Taskforce report published in 2025 acknowledges that women and girls remain significantly underdiagnosed.

Will a private ADHD diagnosis be accepted by my NHS GP?

NHS GP acceptance of a private ADHD diagnosis varies between practices and regions. Assessments conducted to NICE NG87 standards and the UKAAN quality framework, supported by comprehensive multidisciplinary reports, are more likely to be accepted for shared care. It is honest to say this cannot be guaranteed, and the landscape has become more complex as NHS pressures have increased. We discuss shared care during the assessment so you have a realistic picture before making any decisions about ongoing medication management.

What is a clinical formulation and why does it matter?

A clinical formulation goes beyond a binary yes or no diagnosis. It explains how your developmental history, current circumstances, and specific cognitive and emotional patterns interact, giving you an account of the why behind the diagnosis rather than just the label. For an adult who has spent years wondering what is wrong with them, a formulation is often more useful than the diagnostic outcome alone. It also guides treatment planning more precisely, because it identifies which aspects of the presentation are driving the most difficulty in your particular life.

Can anxiety or depression be mistaken for ADHD?

Yes, and the overlap can run in both directions. Anxiety and depression may be correctly identified and treated while the underlying ADHD that is partly driving both goes unrecognised. Equally, anxiety symptoms can develop as a consequence of years of living with unrecognised ADHD. Symptoms of poor concentration, memory difficulties, and low motivation are not specific to ADHD and can arise from anxiety, depression, trauma, burnout, or a combination. A thorough assessment explores this carefully rather than accepting the first explanation that fits.

What happens after an ADHD diagnosis in terms of treatment?

A confirmed diagnosis opens a discussion about management options rather than automatically starting treatment. Medication is considered only where clinically appropriate and after a full discussion of benefits, risks, and alternatives. Where medication is recommended, it is prescribed and titrated by a Consultant Psychiatrist. Psychological support, including CBT approaches adapted for ADHD, is also available and is often recommended alongside or in addition to medication. Ongoing care can be managed by the same clinical team, with medication eventually transferred to a GP under shared care where that arrangement is achievable.
