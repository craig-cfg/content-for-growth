Solar Panels in Yorkshire: How Much Power Will You Actually Generate?

A 4 kW south-facing solar array in West Yorkshire generates approximately 3,100 to 3,300 kilowatt-hours per year under MCS irradiance data for this region. That is less than the national figures you will see quoted on most comparison websites, which are skewed toward southern England. It is still enough to cover a substantial portion of a typical household's electricity use, and it works out to a genuine return on investment. What it is not is a number you should accept without checking it against your specific roof.

Most of the solar generation figures circulating online are built on UK national averages. Those averages are weighted toward the south, where installed capacity is highest and irradiance is strongest. A homeowner in Dewsbury, Leeds, or Sheffield who reads that solar panels generate 4,200 kilowatt-hours per year from a 4 kWp system is looking at a figure that belongs to a roof in Surrey, not West Yorkshire.

We have been installing solar panels across Yorkshire since before the Feed-in Tariff made them fashionable, and the question we hear more than any other at survey stage is some version of: "Does it actually work up here?" The honest answer is yes. But the numbers are different from what the national marketing says, and understanding the difference is the starting point for deciding whether solar is the right call for your home.

What a 4 kW system actually generates in Yorkshire

The standard method for estimating solar generation in the UK is the MCS Standard Estimation Method, set out in the MCS installation standard MIS 3002. Every MCS-certified installer uses it. It calculates output by multiplying your system size in kilowatts-peak by a location-specific irradiance factor, known as the kK value, then adjusting for roof orientation, pitch, and shading.

For West Yorkshire, the MCS irradiance figure sits at approximately 850 to 900 kilowatt-hours per kilowatt-peak per year on a south-facing roof at around 35 to 40 degrees pitch. Applied to a 4 kWp system, that gives an annual generation of approximately 3,100 to 3,300 kilowatt-hours. For a 4.6 kWp system, which is closer to the UK average system size installed in 2024 according to data published alongside MCS irradiance analysis, the range moves to roughly 3,600 to 3,900 kilowatt-hours.

The average UK household uses around 3,400 kilowatt-hours of electricity per year. A properly sized, well-positioned Yorkshire system covers a significant proportion of that. It will not cover all of it, because solar only generates during daylight, and Yorkshire winters are short on both. But the generation is real, predictable, and based on the same methodology your installer uses to quote you.

How system size works in practice

A "4 kWp" system has a peak output of 4 kilowatts under ideal laboratory conditions. In real Yorkshire conditions, it will rarely hit that peak. Annual generation is the number that matters for your finances. The MCS kK value for West Yorkshire accounts for actual average irradiance across the year, not the best-case summer peak.

The factors that shift your generation up or down

The 3,100 to 3,300 kilowatt-hour range assumes a south-facing roof at 35 to 40 degrees pitch with no shading. Most roofs are not quite that. What we see across the Yorkshire homes we survey is a range of orientations, pitches, and shading conditions that each move the generation estimate in a different direction.

Orientation matters more than most buyers expect. A south-facing roof is optimal, but south-east and south-west-facing roofs perform at roughly 95% of that figure. An east or west-facing roof drops to around 80%. If your roof faces north, solar panels will not generate enough to justify the cost. We tell buyers this plainly at survey stage, because fitting panels on a north-facing roof to chase the maximum number of panels is not a service we offer.

Roof pitch has a smaller effect than orientation, and it is also less fixable. The optimal angle for a Yorkshire roof is around 35 to 40 degrees. Most standard pitched roofs in the region fall close to this range. Flat roofs need ballasted mounting frames to tilt the panels, which adds cost and is worth building into your budget from the start.

Shading is the factor buyers most often underestimate. A chimney stack, a dormer window, a large tree, or a neighbouring extension that shadows part of the array for even a few hours per day will reduce annual generation. Modern inverter designs with optimiser technology mitigate this, but they do not eliminate it. We check shading at survey stage using actual roof measurements, not a satellite view.

How generation varies across the year

Yorkshire gets around 1,350 to 1,400 hours of sunshine per year. The distribution is heavily seasonal. In June and July, a well-positioned system will generate three to four times as much electricity per day as it generates in December. This seasonal swing is steeper in the north than in the south of England, and it matters for how you use the electricity the system produces.

In summer, a typical Yorkshire household with a 4 kWp system will generate more electricity than it consumes during the day. That surplus goes to the grid unless you have battery storage. In winter, the system generates less than the household needs for most of the day, and the gap is covered by the grid. The annual generation figure smooths this into a single number, but the seasonal picture tells you more about how the system will actually behave day to day.

The implication is that a system sized for maximum annual generation is not always the most useful system for a specific household. If your electricity use is highest in the morning and evening, a large system generating a big summer surplus you cannot use is less valuable than a smaller system with a battery that shifts generation into the times you actually need it.

What you can actually do with that electricity

Solar electricity has two destinations: your own home, or the grid. Using it yourself is always more valuable. The electricity you consume directly saves you buying from the grid at the current rate, which the Ofgem price cap has set at 24.5 pence per kilowatt-hour as of April 2026. Electricity you export to the grid is paid for through the Smart Export Guarantee, at rates that currently range from around 4 to 15 pence per kilowatt-hour depending on your supplier and tariff choice.

That gap matters. Self-consumed electricity is worth roughly twice as much as exported electricity at current rates. Which means the financial case for solar is built more on reducing your import bill than on maximising export income. This is the calculation that most solar marketing underplays, because "earn money by exporting to the grid" is a more attractive headline than "reduce your electricity spend by using more of what you generate."

Ofgem's SEG Year 5 Annual Report recorded 270,395 installations registered to a Smart Export Guarantee tariff by March 2025, with £56.97 million paid on 443 gigawatt-hours of exported electricity. That works out to an average export payment of around 13 pence per kilowatt-hour across all registered installations. A household achieving 70% self-consumption on a 4 kWp Yorkshire system will export around 900 to 1,000 kilowatt-hours per year. At 13 pence, that is roughly £120 to £130 in annual export income. The bigger financial return is in the electricity you do not have to buy.

The Feed-in Tariff is closed to new applicants

The Feed-in Tariff closed to new applicants in March 2019. If your solar system was installed after that date, you are on the Smart Export Guarantee. FiT paid for both generation and export. SEG pays for export only, at rates set by suppliers rather than the government. If you are researching solar for a new installation, all export income calculations should use SEG rates, not FiT.

How battery storage changes the picture

A solar battery stores surplus generation during the day for use in the evening, when panels are not producing but the household is still consuming electricity. For most Yorkshire homes without battery storage, self-consumption sits around 40 to 50% of annual generation. Add a battery, and that figure typically rises to 70 to 80%, depending on household size and usage pattern.

The financial logic is straightforward: every kilowatt-hour you store and use later saves you 24.5 pence you would otherwise pay the grid for. Every kilowatt-hour you export earns around 4 to 15 pence. Shifting generation from the export column to the self-consumption column is worth double the headline SEG rate on each unit. For a household with high evening electricity use, a battery often pays back its additional cost within the overall system payback period.

The battery size that suits a home depends on the solar array size and the household's daily electricity consumption pattern. Most Yorkshire homes on a standard profile suit a 5 to 10 kilowatt-hour battery. A household running an air source heat pump alongside solar will often benefit from a larger battery, because the heat pump adds a significant overnight and early-morning load that the battery can serve before it recharges from the following day's generation.

Frequently asked questions about solar panels in Yorkshire

How much do solar panels generate in Yorkshire compared to the south of England?

A 4 kWp south-facing system in West Yorkshire generates approximately 3,100 to 3,300 kilowatt-hours per year, based on MCS irradiance data for the region. An identical system in Brighton would generate around 4,500 kilowatt-hours. The gap is real, but it does not make Yorkshire solar unviable. The financial case depends on electricity prices and your self-consumption rate, both of which are the same regardless of location. Yorkshire systems generate less, but the electricity they do generate offsets the same import price as anywhere else in the country.

Is a south-facing roof essential for solar panels in Yorkshire?

South-facing is optimal, but south-east and south-west-facing roofs perform at around 95% of the south-facing figure. East and west-facing roofs drop to around 80% and can still be viable depending on system size and household electricity use. North-facing roofs do not generate enough to justify installation. We check orientation, pitch, and shading at survey stage and will tell you clearly if the system is unlikely to perform as expected. We do not install panels on roofs where the numbers do not work.

What is the Smart Export Guarantee and how much will I earn from it in Yorkshire?

The Smart Export Guarantee is the government-backed scheme requiring large energy suppliers to pay for surplus solar electricity exported to the grid. It replaced the Feed-in Tariff, which closed to new applicants in March 2019. Current rates range from around 4 to 15 pence per kilowatt-hour depending on your supplier, with Octopus Energy offering one of the higher fixed rates at 12 pence as of April 2026. A Yorkshire household with a 4 kWp system achieving 70% self-consumption typically exports around 900 to 1,000 kilowatt-hours per year. At 12 pence per kilowatt-hour, that is approximately £108 to £120 in annual export income. Your MCS installation certificate is required to register.

Does a solar battery make financial sense for a Yorkshire home?

For most households with evening electricity use and a solar system generating more than 3,000 kilowatt-hours per year, a battery improves the financial return by shifting surplus generation from export (worth 4 to 15 pence per kilowatt-hour) to self-consumption (worth 24.5 pence per kilowatt-hour). Whether it makes sense for a specific home depends on the daily electricity consumption pattern. Households that are at home during the day and consume a lot of their solar directly get less benefit from a battery than those with a primarily evening and morning usage profile. We model both scenarios as part of every solar quote.

How do solar panels and an air source heat pump work together in Yorkshire?

Solar and heat pump combinations are among the most effective home energy configurations available to Yorkshire homeowners. The heat pump adds substantial electricity demand, typically 3,000 to 5,000 kilowatt-hours per year depending on home size and heat loss. A solar array sized to offset part of that demand directly reduces the heat pump's running cost. The most effective configuration pairs a 4 to 6 kWp solar array with a 10 kilowatt-hour battery, which handles overnight heat pump operation from stored generation rather than grid electricity. We design both as a single system because the sizing decisions interact. Installing solar and a heat pump separately, or at different times, usually means one or both systems is undersized for the combined profile.

Do I need planning permission for solar panels in Yorkshire?

Most residential solar installations in England fall within permitted development rights and do not require planning permission, provided they meet certain conditions: the panels must not project more than 200 millimetres beyond the roof plane, they must not be fitted to a wall or roof that faces a highway, and they must not be installed on a listed building or in certain designated areas. Properties in conservation areas, national parks, or areas of outstanding natural beauty may have additional restrictions.
